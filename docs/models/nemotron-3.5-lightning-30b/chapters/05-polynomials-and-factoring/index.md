## Polynomials and Their Parts

Polynomials are algebraic expressions consisting of variables and coefficients, combined using addition, subtraction, and multiplication. A polynomial can have one term (a monomial), two terms (a binomial), or three terms (a trinomial). The **degree** of a polynomial is the highest power of the variable present, and the **leading coefficient** is the coefficient of the term with the highest degree.

### Special Product

A **special product** is a polynomial product that follows a predictable pattern, allowing for faster multiplication without distributing each term individually. The most common special products are the difference of squares and perfect square trinomials.

- **Difference of squares:** \((a + b)(a - b) = a^2 - b^2\)
- **Perfect square trinomial:** \((a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2\) and \((a - b)^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2\)

These patterns are useful because they appear frequently in algebra and higher mathematics, and recognizing them saves time and reduces errors.

### Polynomial Degree

The **degree** of a polynomial is the highest exponent of the variable in the expression. For a single-term polynomial (monomial), the degree is the exponent on the variable. For a polynomial with multiple terms, the degree is the highest degree among all terms.

**Worked example:** Find the degree of \(4x^3 - 2x^2 + 7x - 5\).

- The first term \(4x^3\) has degree 3
- The second term \(-2x^2\) has degree 2
- The third term \(7x\) has degree 1
- The fourth term \(-5\) has degree 0 (constant)

The highest degree is 3, so the polynomial has degree 3.

### Like Terms

**Like terms** are terms that have the same variable raised to the same power. Only the coefficients can differ. When combining like terms, we add or subtract the coefficients while keeping the variable part unchanged.

For example, \(3x^2\) and \(-5x^2\) are like terms because both contain \(x^2\). Their sum is \((3 + (-5))x^2 = -2x^2\).

Unlike terms, such as \(3x^2\) and \(5x\), cannot be combined because the variable parts are different.

### Unlike Terms

**Unlike terms** are terms that have different variable parts — either different variables or the same variable raised to different powers. Unlike terms cannot be combined through addition or subtraction.

For example, \(3x^2\) and \(5x\) are unlike terms because one has \(x^2\) and the other has \(x\). Similarly, \(2xy\) and \(3x\) are unlike terms because the first contains both \(x\) and \(y\) while the second contains only \(x\).

Recognizing unlike terms is just as important as recognizing like terms when simplifying polynomial expressions.

### Standard Form Polynomial

A polynomial is in **standard form** when its terms are written in descending order of degree, from highest to lowest. The leading coefficient is positioned first, followed by terms of decreasing degree, and the constant term comes last.

For example, the polynomial \(3x^2 + 7 - 4x^3 + x\) in non-standard form becomes \(-4x^3 + 3x^2 + x + 7\) in standard form.

Writing polynomials in standard form makes it easy to identify the degree and leading coefficient at a glance.

### Leading Coefficient

The **leading coefficient** is the coefficient of the term with the highest degree in a polynomial written in standard form. It determines the overall direction and steepness of the polynomial's graph.

In the polynomial \(-3x^4 + 2x^3 - x + 7\), the leading coefficient is \(-3\). The sign of the leading coefficient affects the end behavior of the polynomial graph: a positive leading coefficient typically means the graph rises to the right, while a negative leading coefficient means the graph falls to the right.

### Constant Term Polynomial

The **constant term** is the term in a polynomial that does not contain a variable — it is purely a number. In standard form, the constant term is always written last.

In the polynomial \(2x^3 - 4x^2 + 5x - 8\), the constant term is \(-8\). The constant term represents the value of the polynomial when the variable equals zero, and it corresponds to the y-intercept of the graph when the polynomial is written in standard form.

## Quadratic Functions and Their Forms

A **quadratic function** is a polynomial of degree 2, typically written in the form \(f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c\), where \(a \neq 0\). The graph of a quadratic function is a parabola. Understanding the different forms of quadratic functions makes it easier to identify key features such as the vertex, axis of symmetry, and intercepts.

### Vertex Form Quadratic

The **vertex form** of a quadratic function is \(f(x) = a(x - h)^2 + k\), where \((h, k)\) is the vertex of the parabola and the axis of symmetry is the line \(x = h\). The parameter \(a\) affects the width and direction of the parabola: if \(a > 0\), the parabola opens upward; if \(a < 0\), it opens downward.

**Worked example:** Graph \(f(x) = 2(x - 3)^2 - 4\) and identify the vertex and direction of opening.

- The vertex is \((h, k) = (3, -4)\)
- Since \(a = 2 > 0\), the parabola opens upward
- The axis of symmetry is \(x = 3\)

To graph, start at the vertex \((3, -4)\) and plot additional points by choosing \(x\)-values around the vertex, then connect them with a smooth parabolic curve.

### Intercept Form Quadratic

The **intercept form** (or factored form) of a quadratic function is \(f(x) = a(x - p)(x - q)\), where \(p\) and \(q\) are the \(x\)-intercepts (roots or zeros) of the function. The parameter \(a\) still affects the width and direction of the parabola.

**Worked example:** Write the quadratic function with \(x\)-intercepts at \(x = -2\) and \(x = 5\) that passes through the point \((1, 12)\).

Using the intercept form: \(f(x) = a(x - (-2))(x - 5) = a(x + 2)(x - 5)\)

Substitute \(x = 1\) and \(f(1) = 12\):
\(12 = a(1 + 2)(1 - 5) = a(3)(-4) = -12a\)
\(a = -1\)

So the function is \(f(x) = -(x + 2)(x - 5) = -(x^2 - 3x - 10) = -x^2 + 3x + 10\).

### Quadratic Maximum

When the leading coefficient \(a < 0\) in a quadratic function, the parabola opens **downward**, and the vertex represents the **maximum** value of the function. The \(y\)-coordinate of the vertex is the maximum value of \(y\) for the given quadratic.

**Worked example:** Find the maximum value of \(f(x) = -2(x - 3)^2 + 5\).

Since \(a = -2 < 0\), the parabola opens downward, so the vertex gives the maximum value. The vertex is \((h, k) = (3, 5)\), so the maximum value of \(f(x)\) is 5, achieved when \(x = 3\).

### Quadratic Minimum

When the leading coefficient \(a > 0\) in a quadratic function, the parabola opens **upward**, and the vertex represents the **minimum** value of the function. The \(y\)-coordinate of the vertex is the minimum value of \(y\) for the given quadratic.

**Worked example:** Find the minimum value of \(f(x) = 3(x + 1)^2 - 4\).

Since \(a = 3 > 0\), the parabola opens upward, so the vertex gives the minimum value. The vertex is \((h, k) = (-1, -4)\), so the minimum value of \(f(x)\) is \(-4\), achieved when \(x = -1\).

## Exponential Functions

An **exponential function** has the form \(f(x) = ab^x\), where \(a \neq 0\), \(b > 0\), and \(b \neq 1\). The base \(b\) determines whether the function represents growth (\(b > 1\)) or decay (\(0 < b < 1\)). Exponential functions model many real-world phenomena, including population growth, radioactive decay, and compound interest.

### Exponential Growth

**Exponential growth** occurs when a quantity increases by a constant percentage over equal time intervals. The base \(b > 1\) in the exponential function \(f(x) = ab^x\) represents the growth factor.

**Worked example:** A bacterial culture doubles every hour. If the initial population is 500 bacteria, write a function for the population after \(t\) hours and find the population after 3 hours.

The function is \(f(t) = 500(2)^t\). After 3 hours: \(f(3) = 500(2)^3 = 500(8) = 4000\) bacteria.

### Exponential Decay

**Exponential decay** occurs when a quantity decreases by a constant percentage over equal time intervals. The base \(0 < b < 1\) in the exponential function \(f(x) = ab^x\) represents the decay factor.

**Worked example:** A radioactive substance loses 10% of its mass each year. If the initial mass is 200 grams, write a function for the remaining mass after \(t\) years and find the mass after 3 years.

The decay factor is \(b = 1 - 0.10 = 0.90\). The function is \(f(t) = 200(0.90)^t\). After 3 years: \(f(3) = 200(0.90)^3 = 200(0.729) = 145.8\) grams remaining.

### Exponential Base

The **base** \(b\) of an exponential function \(f(x) = ab^x\) determines the rate of growth or decay:
- If \(b > 1\), the function represents exponential growth
- If \(0 < b < 1\), the function represents exponential decay
- The distance of \(b\) from 1 indicates the rate of increase or decrease

**Worked example:** Compare the functions \(f(x) = 100(1.05)^x\) and \(g(x) = 100(0.95)^x\). Which represents growth and which represents decay? What are the growth/decay rates?

- \(f(x) = 100(1.05)^x\): growth base \(b = 1.05\), so the growth rate is \(5\%\) per unit increase in \(x\)
- \(g(x) = 100(0.95)^x\): decay base \(b = 0.95\), so the decay rate is \(5\%\) per unit increase in \(x\)

### Exponent Growth Factor

The **exponent growth factor** is the base \(b\) in an exponential growth function \(f(x) = ab^x\) where \(b > 1\). It represents the multiplicative factor by which the quantity increases for each unit increase in the exponent.

**Worked example:** If a population grows by a factor of 1.5 each year, starting with 100 individuals, the population after 4 years is \(100(1.5)^4 = 100(5.0625) = 506.25\) individuals.

### Exponent Decay Factor

The **exponent decay factor** is the base \(b\) in an exponential decay function \(f(x) = ab^x\) where \(0 < b < 1\). It represents the multiplicative factor by which the quantity decreases for each unit increase in the exponent.

**Worked example:** If a substance decays by a factor of 0.8 each day, starting with 100 grams, the remaining amount after 3 days is \(100(0.8)^3 = 100(0.512) = 51.2\) grams.

### Scientific Notation

**Scientific notation** is a way of writing very large or very small numbers in a compact form as a product of a number between 1 and 10 and a power of 10. The format is \(m \times 10^n\), where \(1 \leq |m| < 10\) and \(n\) is an integer.

**Worked example:** Write \(450,000\) in scientific notation.

Move the decimal point 5 places to the left: \(4.5 \times 10^5\).

Write \(0.00003\) in scientific notation.

Move the decimal point 5 places to the right: \(3 \times 10^{-5}\).

## Key Takeaways

- Special products like difference of squares and perfect square trinomials follow predictable patterns that speed up polynomial multiplication.
- The degree of a polynomial is the highest exponent of the variable; standard form arranges terms in descending order of degree.
- Like terms have identical variable parts and can be combined; unlike terms cannot.
- Quadratic functions have three common forms: standard, vertex, and intercept form, each highlighting different features.
- When a < 0 in a quadratic, the vertex is a maximum; when a > 0, the vertex is a minimum.
- Exponential growth uses a base b > 1; exponential decay uses a base 0 < b < 1.
- Scientific notation simplifies writing and working with extremely large or small numbers.
