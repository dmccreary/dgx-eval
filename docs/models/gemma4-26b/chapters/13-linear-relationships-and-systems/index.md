## Detailed Content

Algebra is more than just finding $x$; it is about describing how things change and relate to one another. In this chapter, we will see how simple ideas like ratios and fractions evolve into the powerful tools of linear and quadratic equations, allowing us to model everything from the path of a ball to the growth of a population.

### Ratios

A ratio is a comparison of two quantities. For example, if you have 3 apples and 2 oranges, the ratio of apples to oranges is 3 to 2, often written as $3:2$ or $\frac{3}{2}$. Ratios help us understand the relationship between different parts of a whole or between two separate groups.

### Fractions

Fractions represent parts of a whole. When we work with algebraic expressions, we often encounter fractions that require careful manipulation.

**Worked Example: Multiplying Fractions**

To multiply two fractions, multiply their numerators together and their denominators together.
Suppose we want to find $\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{4}{5}$.
1. Multiply the numerators: $2 \times 4 = 8$.
2. Multiply the denominators: $3 \times 5 = 15$.
The result is $\frac{8}{15}$.

### Combining Like Terms

In algebra, "like terms" are terms that have the exact same variable part. For example, $3x$ and $5x$ are like terms, but $3x$ and $3x^2$ are not. To combine like terms, you add or subtract their coefficients. For $3x + 5x$, the result is $8x$.

### Expanding Expressions

Expanding an expression means removing parentheses by multiplying the terms inside by the term outside. This is often done using the distributive property. For instance, expanding $3(x + 2)$ results in $3x + 6$.

### Greatest Common Factor

The Greatest Common Factor (GCF) is the largest number or term that divides evenly into two or more terms. For $6x$ and $9$, the GCF is $3$. In algebra, finding the GCF is a crucial first step in factoring.

### Adding Polynomials

A polynomial is an expression consisting of variables and coefficients. To add polynomials, you simply combine like terms from both expressions. For example, $(2x + 3) + (x - 1)$ becomes $3x + 2$.

### Solving by Factoring

Factoring is the process of breaking an expression down into a product of simpler expressions. This is particularly useful for solving equations.

**Worked Example: Factoring a Trinomial**

To solve $x^2 + 5x + 6 = 0$, we look for two numbers that multiply to $6$ and add to $5$. These numbers are $2$ and $3$.
So, we can rewrite the equation as:
\[ (x + 2)(x + 3) = 0 \]
This gives us two possible solutions: $x = -2$ or $x = -3$.

### Quadratic Equations

A quadratic equation is an equation where the highest power of the variable is 2, such as $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$. These equations describe parabolic shapes.

**Worked Example: Solving via Quadratic Formula**

For the equation $x^2 - 4x + 3 = 0$, where $a=1, b=-4, c=3$:
Using the formula \( x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a} \):
\[ x = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{(-4)^2 - 4(1)(3)}}{2(1)} \]
\[ x = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{16 - 12}}{2} \]
\[ x = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{4}}{2} = \frac{4 \pm 2}{2} \]
\( x = 3 \) or \( x = 1 \).

### Quadratic Models

Quadratic models use quadratic equations to represent real-world phenomena, such as the trajectory of a projectile. The resulting graph is a parabola, which can open upward or downward.

### Standard Form

The standard form of a linear equation is \( Ax + By = C \). This form is useful for finding intercepts and is often used in systems of equations.

| Form Name | Equation | Best Use Case |
|-----------|----------|---------------|
| Slope-Intercept | \( y = mx + b \) | Graphing |
| Point-Slope | \( y - y_1 = m(x - x_1) \) | Writing eq from a point |
| Standard Form | \( Ax + By = C \) | Finding intercepts |

### Point-Slope Form

The point-slope form of a linear equation is \( y - y_1 = m(x - x_1) \), where \( m \) is the slope and \( (x_1, y_1) \) is a point on the line. This is a very efficient way to write the equation of a line when you know its steepness and one specific location.

### Graphing Linear Equations

Graphing a linear equation involves plotting points on a coordinate plane that satisfy the equation. One common method is to find the x-intercept and y-intercept and then draw a line through them.

### Exponential Models

Exponential models describe situations where a quantity grows or decays at a constant percentage rate, such as population growth or compound interest. These are represented by equations like \( y = ab^x \).

### Line of Best Fit

A line of best fit is a straight line drawn through a set of data points on a scatterplot to represent the general trend. It is often found using linear regression.
