## Foundations of Reasoning

Before we can model complex systems, we must establish a clear framework for how we reason about them.

### Mathematical Logic

**Mathematical logic** is the study of formal reasoning and the principles that govern valid arguments. In algebra, we use logic to determine if an equation is true, if a solution is valid, or if a statement about a function's behavior is universally applicable. It allows us to move from "guessing" to "proving" that a mathematical model accurately represents a given set of conditions. For example, using logical deduction, we can prove that if \(x^2 = 4\), then \(x\) must be either \(2\) or \(-2\).

### Basic Geometry

While algebra often focuses on symbols, it is deeply intertwined with **basic geometry**. Geometry provides the visual and spatial context for many algebraic concepts. For example, when we solve for a variable in a geometric formula, such as finding the radius of a circle given its area, we are using algebra to describe physical properties. Understanding concepts such as points, lines, angles, and shapes is essential for interpreting the graphs that represent algebraic functions.

### Proportions

A **proportion** is an equation that states that two ratios are equal. For instance, if one object is twice as large as another, the ratio of their lengths is \(2:1\). In modeling, proportions allow us to scale relationships—such as predicting how much a population will grow based on a constant rate of increase. Understanding how different quantities relate to each other through constant ratios is a cornerstone of both algebra and statistical analysis.

### Percentages

**Percentages** are a specific way of expressing proportions where the denominator is always 100. They are a vital tool for describing changes and comparing values. In statistics, percentages are used to describe the distribution of data, the probability of an event, or the margin of error in a model. Mastery of percentages allows us to translate abstract algebraic ratios into the familiar language of real-world data, such as a \(15\%\) increase in revenue.

## Polynomial Operations

Moving into more complex expressions, we encounter polynomials, which are the building blocks of higher-level modeling.

### Multiplying Polynomials

**Multiplying polynomials** is the process of finding the product of two or more algebraic expressions. This often involves using the distributive property to ensure every term in the first polynomial is multiplied by every term in the second. For example, when we multiply \((x + 2)(x + 3)\), we are finding a single expression that represents the combined effect of these two factors. This expansion is a fundamental step in simplifying complex mathematical models.

### Subtracting Polynomials

**Subtracting polynomials** involves finding the difference between two algebraic expressions. To do this correctly, we must distribute the negative sign to every term in the polynomial being subtracted and then combine like terms. This is a critical skill when comparing two different models or finding the change in a quantity over time, such as calculating the change in velocity between two time points.

### Factoring Expressions

**Factoring expressions** is the inverse process of multiplication. It involves breaking down a polynomial into a product of simpler expressions, known as factors. For example, if we know the product is \(x^2 + 5x + 6\), factoring allows us to find that the original components were \((x + 2)\) and \((x + 3)\). Factoring is essential for solving equations, finding roots, and simplifying complex models for easier analysis.

## Exploring Quadratic Functions

Polynomials of a certain degree lead us into the study of quadratics, which describe many natural phenomena, such as the path of a projectile or the shape of a satellite dish.

### Quadratic Functions

A **quadratic function** is a polynomial function of the second degree, typically written in the form \(f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c\), where \(a \neq 0\). Unlike linear functions, which represent constant change, quadratic functions represent changing rates of change. This allows them to model accelerating or decelerating processes, such as the way gravity affects a falling object or the spread of a viral infection.

### Vertex of a Parabola

When we graph a quadratic function, it forms a U-shaped curve called a **parabola**. The **vertex of a parabola** is the most significant point on this curve; it represents either the absolute maximum or the absolute minimum value of the function. In a real-world model, such as a ball being thrown, the vertex represents the highest point the ball reaches before it begins its descent.

### Axis of Symmetry

Every parabola is perfectly balanced on both sides of a central line called the **axis of symmetry**. This vertical line passes directly through the vertex and divides the parabola into two mirror-image halves. The equation for this line is always related to the \(x\)-coordinate of the vertex, providing a structural anchor for the function's graph and helping us understand its symmetrical nature.

### Graphing Quadratics

**Graphing quadratics** is the process of visually representing a quadratic function on a coordinate plane. By identifying key points—such as the vertex, the \(y\)-intercept, and the \(x\)-intercepts (also known as roots)—we can sketch the characteristic U-shape of the parabola. This visual representation is crucial for understanding the behavior, limits, and real-world implications of the mathematical model.

### Completing the Square

**Completing the square** is a technique used to transform a quadratic expression from its standard form, \(ax^2 + bx + c\), into its vertex form, \(a(x - h)^2 + k\). This process involves adding and subtracting a specific constant to create a perfect square trinomial. This form is incredibly useful because it makes the vertex \((h, k)\) immediately apparent, which is helpful for analyzing the maximum or minimum of a function.

#### Diagram: Completing the Square Process

**Worked Example:**
To complete the square for the expression \(x^2 + 6x + 5\):

1. **Identify the \(b\) term:** In this expression, \(b = 6\).
2. **Find the value to add:** Take half of \(b\) and square it: \((\frac{6}{2})^2 = 3^2 = 9\).
3. **Add and subtract this value:** 
   \[ x^2 + 6x + 9 - 9 + 5 \]
4. **Group the perfect square:**
   \[ (x^2 + 6x + 9) - 9 + 5 \]
5. **Simplify:**
   \[ (x + 3)^2 - 4 \]

The vertex of this parabola is \((-3, -4)\).

### The Quadratic Formula

When a quadratic equation cannot be easily factored, we use the **quadratic formula** to find its roots. The formula, \(x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}\), provides a direct way to calculate the values of \(x\) that make the equation \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\) true. The part under the square root, \(b^2 - 4ac\), is called the discriminant and determines whether the roots are real or complex.

#### Diagram: Quadratic Formula Components

| Component | Role in Formula |
|-----------|-----------------|
| \(a\) | Leading coefficient |
| \(b\) | Linear coefficient |
| \(c\) | Constant term |
| \(b^2 - 4ac\) | Discriminant |

**Worked Example:**
Solve the equation \(2x^2 - 5x + 3 = 0\).

1. **Identify the coefficients:** \(a = 2\), \(b = -5\), \(c = 3\).
2. **Apply the formula:**
   \[ x = \frac{-(-5) \pm \sqrt{(-5)^2 - 4(2)(3)}}{2(2)} \]
3. **Simplify the discriminant:**
   \[ x = \frac{5 \pm \sqrt{25 - 24}}{4} \]
   \[ x = \frac{5 \pm \sqrt{1}}{4} \]
4. **Calculate the two solutions:**
   \[ x_1 = \frac{5 + 1}{4} = \frac{6}{4} = 1.5 \]
   \[ x_2 = \frac{5 - 1}{4} = \frac{4}{4} = 1 \]

The solutions are \(x = 1.5\) and \(x = 1\).

## Statistical Modeling

Having mastered the manipulation of algebraic expressions and functions, we can now apply these tools to analyze real-world data.

### Linear Regression

**Linear regression** is a statistical method used to model the relationship between a dependent variable and one or more independent variables by fitting a linear equation to observed data. In our algebraic context, we use regression to find the "line of best fit" through a scatterplot of data points. This line serves as a mathematical model, allowing us to predict future trends or describe the underlying relationship between variables, such as how study time might correlate with test scores.
