### Terms in an Expression

Before we can perform complex algebra, we must understand the fundamental building blocks of any algebraic expression. An algebraic expression is a mathematical phrase that can contain numbers, variables, and operation symbols.

The basic unit of an expression is called a **term**. A term can be a single number (called a constant), a single variable, or a combination of numbers and variables multiplied together. For example, in the expression \( 4x^2 - 3y + 7 \), the terms are \( 4x^2 \), \( -3y \), and \( 7 \). Note that the sign (positive or negative) is attached to the term.

#### Worked Example: Identifying Terms

Consider the expression:
\[ 5a + 2b - 9 \]

1. The first term is \( 5a \), which consists of the coefficient \( 5 \) and the variable \( a \).
2. The second term is \( 2b \), consisting of the coefficient \( 2 \) and the variable \( b \).
3. The third term is \( -9 \), which is a constant term.

Each of these terms is separated by an addition or subtraction sign.

### Combining Like Terms

Once we can identify individual terms, we can begin to simplify expressions by **combining like terms**. 

Two or more terms are considered **like terms** if they have the exact same variables raised to the exact same exponents. The coefficients (the numbers in front of the variables) do not need to be the same. For instance, \( 7x^2 \) and \( -2x^2 \) are like terms, but \( 7x^2 \) and \( 7x \) are not, because the exponents are different.

#### Worked Example: Simplifying an Expression

Simplify the following expression:
\[ 8x + 5y - 3x + 2y + 4 \]

**Step 1: Group the like terms.**
\[ (8x - 3x) + (5y + 2y) + 4 \]

**Step 2: Combine the coefficients of the like terms.**
\[ (8 - 3)x + (5 + 2)y + 4 \]
\[ 5x + 7y + 4 \]

The simplified expression is \( 5x + 7y + 4 \).

### Polynomials

We can now expand our view to a special class of expressions called **polynomials**. A polynomial is a mathematical expression made up of one or more terms, where each term consists of a coefficient multiplied by a variable raised to a non-negative integer power.

Polynomials are classified by the number of terms they contain:
- **Monomial:** A polynomial with exactly one term (e.g., \( 5x^3 \)).
- **Binomial:** A polynomial with exactly two terms (e.g., \( 2x + 3 \)).
- **Trinomial:** A polynomial with exactly three terms (e.g., \( x^2 - 4x + 4 \)).

The **degree** of a polynomial is the highest exponent found in any of its terms. For example, in the polynomial \( 4x^3 - 2x + 7 \), the degree is 3.

#### Worked Example: Adding Polynomials

When adding polynomials, we follow the same process as combining like terms.

Simplify: \( (3x^2 + 5x - 4) + (x^2 - 2x + 7) \)

**Step 1: Remove the parentheses.**
\[ 3x^2 + 5x - 4 + x^2 - 2x + 7 \]

**Step 2: Group the like terms.**
\[ (3x^2 + x^2) + (5x - 2x) + (-4 + 7) \]

**Step 3: Combine the coefficients.**
\[ 4x^2 + 3x + 3 \]

The sum is \( 4x^2 + 3x + 3 \).

### Product Rule of Exponents

When we multiply two powers that have the same base, we can simplify the result using the **Product Rule of Exponents**.

The rule states that for any non-zero base \( x \) and any exponents \( a \) and \( b \):
\[ x^a \cdot x^b = x^{a+b} \]

Essentially, when you multiply terms with the same base, you keep the base and add the exponents.

#### Worked Example: Applying the Product Rule

Simplify: \( y^4 \cdot y^7 \)

**Step 1: Identify the base and the exponents.**
Base: \( y \)
Exponents: \( 4 \) and \( 7 \)

**Step 2: Apply the rule by adding the exponents.**
\[ y^{4+7} \]
\[ y^{11} \]

The simplified result is \( y^{11} \).

### Exponential Base

In an exponential expression, the **base** is the number that is being multiplied by itself multiple times. The number of times it is multiplied is known as the **exponent**.

For example, in the expression \( 5^3 \), the number 5 is the base, and 3 is the exponent. This means \( 5^3 = 5 \cdot 5 \cdot 5 = 125 \).

#### Worked Example: Identifying the Base

Identify the base and exponent in the following expression:
\[ 10^6 \]

- **Base:** 10
- **Exponent:** 6

The expression represents 10 multiplied by itself 6 times.

### Rational Exponents

Sometimes, we encounter exponents that are not whole numbers. These are called **rational exponents**. A rational exponent is a fraction, and it represents a combination of a power and a root.

The rule for rational exponents is:
\[ x^{m/n} = \sqrt[n]{x^m} \]

In this notation, \( n \) is the index of the root, and \( m \) is the power.

#### Worked Example: Simplifying a Rational Exponent

Simplify: \( 27^{2/3} \)

**Step 1: Rewrite the expression using the radical form.**
\[ \sqrt[3]{27^2} \quad \text{or} \quad (\sqrt[3]{27})^2 \]

**Step 2: Calculate the cube root first (it is usually easier).**
The cube root of 27 is 3 (because \( 3 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 = 27 \)).
\[ (3)^2 \]

**Step 3: Square the result.**
\[ 3^2 = 9 \]

The simplified value of \( 27^{2/3} \) is 9.

### Simplifying Radicals

To work with roots more efficiently, we often need to **simplify radicals**. Simplifying a radical means rewriting it so that the number under the radical sign (the radicand) has no perfect power factors.

For example, \( \sqrt{12} \) can be simplified because 12 has a perfect square factor, 4.

#### Worked Example: Simplifying a Square Root

Simplify: \( \sqrt{50} \)

**Step 1: Find the largest perfect square factor of 50.**
The factors of 50 are 1, 2, 5, 10, 25, 50.
The largest perfect square is 25.

**Step 2: Rewrite the radical as a product.**
\[ \sqrt{25 \cdot 2} \]

**Step 3: Apply the product rule for radicals.**
\[ \sqrt{25} \cdot \sqrt{2} \]
\[ 5\sqrt{2} \]

The simplified form is \( 5\sqrt{2} \).

### Linear Inequalities

While equations tell us when two expressions are equal, **linear inequalities** tell us when one expression is greater than, less than, or equal to another.

A linear inequality uses one of the following symbols:
- \( > \) : greater than
- \( < \) : less than
- \( \ge \) : greater than or equal to
- \( \le \) : less than or equal to

#### Example: A Simple Inequality

The expression \( x + 5 > 10 \) means that the value of \( x \) plus 5 must be strictly larger than 10. Solving this, we find \( x > 5 \).

### Input and Output

In algebra, we often study how one quantity changes in response to another. This is the core of the concept of a **function**.

A function is a relationship where every **input** (the independent variable, often \( x \)) produces exactly one **output** (the dependent variable, often \( y \) or \( f(x) \)).

#### Worked Example: Evaluating a Function

Given the function:
\[ f(x) = 3x - 2 \]

Find the output when the input is 4.

**Step 1: Substitute the input value into the function.**
\[ f(4) = 3(4) - 2 \]

**Step 2: Perform the arithmetic.**
\[ f(4) = 12 - 2 \]
\[ f(4) = 10 \]

When the input is 4, the output is 10.

### Point-Slope Form

When we want to write the equation of a straight line, there are several ways to do it. One of the most useful forms is the **point-slope form**.

The point-slope form of a linear equation is:
\[ y - y_1 = m(x - x_1) \]

Where:
- \( m \) is the **slope** of the line.
- \( (x_1, y_1) \) is a specific **point** on the line.

This form is particularly helpful when you know one point on the line and how steep the line is.

#### Worked Example: Writing an Equation

Find the equation of the line that passes through the point (2, 5) and has a slope of 3.

**Step 1: Identify the given values.**
\( m = 3 \)
\( x_1 = 2 \)
\( y_1 = 5 \)

**Step 2: Substitute these into the point-slope formula.**
\[ y - 5 = 3(x - 2) \]

The equation in point-slope form is \( y - 5 = 3(x - 2) \).

### Systems of Equations

Sometimes, we need to find a value that satisfies two or more equations at the same time. This is known as a **system of equations**. The solution to the system is the point (or points) where the graphs of the equations intersect.

#### Example: A Simple System

Consider this system:
\[ y = x + 1 \]
\[ y = 2x - 1 \]

To solve this, we can find where the two lines meet. By setting them equal to each other (\( x + 1 = 2x - 1 \)), we find that \( x = 2 \). Plugging \( x = 2 \) back into either equation gives \( y = 3 \). So the solution is the point (2, 3).

### Tabular Representation

A **tabular representation** is a way to show the relationship between variables using a table. Each row in the table represents a pair of input and output values. This is a great way to organize data before graphing it or finding a mathematical model.

#### Worked Example: Creating a Table

Create a table for the function \( f(x) = 2x + 3 \) for the inputs \( x = -1, 0, 1, 2 \).

**Step 1: Calculate the output for each input.**
- For \( x = -1 \): \( f(-1) = 2(-1) + 3 = 1 \)
- For \( x = 0 \): \( f(0) = 2(0) + 3 = 3 \)
- For \( x = 1 \): \( f(1) = 2(1) + 3 = 5 \)
- For \( x = 2 \): \( f(2) = 2(2) + 3 = 7 \)

**Step 2: Organize into a table.**

| Input (\( x \)) | Output (\( f(x) \)) |
|-----------------|---------------------|
| -1              | 1                   |
| 0               | 3                   |
| 1               | 5                   |
| 2               | 7                   |

### Correlation

In statistics, we often look for patterns in how two variables change together. This relationship is called **correlation**.

If one variable increases as the other increases, they have a **positive correlation**. If one increases as the other decreases, they have a **negative correlation**. If there is no apparent pattern, they have **no correlation**.

#### Worked Example: Identifying Correlation

Imagine you collect data on the number of hours spent studying and the test scores achieved by a group of students.

- If students who study more tend to have higher scores, this is a **positive correlation**.
- If students who study more tend to have lower scores (which is unlikely!), this would be a **negative correlation**.
- If there is no relationship between study time and score, it is **no correlation**.

### Arithmetic Sequences

A pattern of numbers can often be described using a mathematical sequence. An **arithmetic sequence** is a special kind of sequence where the difference between any two consecutive terms is always the same. This constant difference is called the **common difference**.

#### Worked Example: Identifying an Arithmetic Sequence

Consider the following sequence:
\[ 5, 12, 19, 26, 33, \dots \]

**Step 1: Find the difference between consecutive terms.**
- \( 12 - 5 = 7 \)
- \( 19 - 12 = 7 \)
- \( 26 - 9 = 7 \)

**Step 2: Check if the difference is constant.**
Since the difference is always 7, this is an arithmetic sequence with a common difference of 7.
