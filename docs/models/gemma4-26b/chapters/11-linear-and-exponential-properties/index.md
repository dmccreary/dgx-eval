## Integers

Before we can manipulate complex algebraic expressions, we must have a firm grasp of the number system we are working within. One of the most fundamental sets of numbers used in algebra is the set of **integers**.

Integers are a collection of whole numbers that include positive numbers, negative numbers, and zero. Unlike natural numbers, which start at 1, integers extend infinitely in both the positive and negative directions on a number line. In algebraic expressions, integers often serve as coefficients or constants that define the magnitude and direction of a relationship.

For example, in the expression \( -5x + 3 \), the numbers \(-5\) and \(3\) are integers. The negative sign on the \(5\) indicates its position on the number line relative to zero.

#### Worked Example: Adding Integers
To understand how integers interact, let's examine an addition problem involving both positive and negative values.

Suppose we want to calculate the sum of \( -7 \) and \( 4 \). We can visualize this on a number line:
1. Start at \( -7 \).
2. Move \( 4 \) units to the right (the positive direction).
3. We land on \( -3 \).

Mathematically, we write this as:
\[ -7 + 4 = -3 \]

This shows that when we add a positive integer to a negative integer, the result's sign depends on which integer has the larger absolute value.

## Identity Property

In algebra, we often perform operations that leave a number unchanged. This phenomenon is known as the **identity property**. There are two main types of identities: the additive identity and the multiplicative identity.

The **additive identity** is the number \( 0 \). When any number is added to zero, the value of the number remains exactly the same. For any real number \( a \), the property is expressed as:
\[ a + 0 = a \]

The **multiplicative identity** is the number \( 1 \). When any number is multiplied by one, the value of the number remains unchanged. For any real number \( a \), the property is expressed as:
\[ a \cdot 1 = a \]

These properties are crucial when simplifying equations or isolating variables, as they allow us to manipulate terms without altering their fundamental value.

## Distributive Property

The **distributive property** is a rule that relates multiplication and addition (or subtraction). It states that multiplying a sum by a number is the same as multiplying each addend by that number and then adding the products together.

In symbolic terms, for any real numbers \( a \), \( b \), and \( c \):
\[ a(b + c) = ab + ac \]

This property is essential for expanding algebraic expressions and for factoring them later. It allows us to "distribute" the factor outside the parentheses to every term inside.

#### Worked Example: Expanding an Expression
Let's apply the distributive property to expand the expression \( 3(x + 5) \).

1. Distribute the \( 3 \) to the first term, \( x \):
   \[ 3 \cdot x = 3x \]
2. Distribute the \( 3 \) to the second term, \( 5 \):
   \[ 3 \cdot 5 = 15 \]
3. Combine the results:
   \[ 3(x + 5) = 3x + 15 \]

By using this property, we have transformed a product into a sum of terms, which is often easier to work with when solving equations.

## Exponents and the Negative Exponent Rule

When we multiply a number by itself multiple times, we use **exponents**. An exponent (or power) tells us how many times the base number is used as a factor.

While we often think of exponents as growing larger, there is a specific rule for when the exponent is a negative integer. The **negative exponent rule** states that a base raised to a negative power is equal to the reciprocal of that base raised to the positive power.

Specifically, for any non-zero number \( a \) and any positive integer \( n \):
\[ a^{-n} = \frac{1}{a^n} \]

This rule is a way of representing division by that base repeated \( n \) times. It ensures that the laws of exponents remain consistent even when moving from growth to decay.

#### Worked Example: Applying Negative Exponents
Let's simplify the expression \( 5^{-2} \).

1. Identify the base and the exponent: The base is \( 5 \) and the exponent is \( -2 \).
2. Apply the negative exponent rule:
   \[ 5^{-2} = \frac{1}{5^2} \]
3. Evaluate the power in the denominator:
   \[ 5^2 = 5 \cdot 5 = 25 \]
4. Final result:
   \[ 5^{-2} = \frac{1}{25} \]

## Scientific Notation

In science and advanced mathematics, we often encounter numbers that are extremely large or extremely small. To make these numbers easier to read and manipulate, we use **scientific notation**.

Scientific notation expresses a number as the product of a coefficient (between 1 and 10) and a power of 10. The form is:
\[ a \times 10^n \]
where \( 1 \le |a| < 10 \) and \( n \) is an integer.

A positive exponent \( n \) indicates a very large number (moving the decimal to the right), while a negative exponent \( n \) indicates a very small number (moving the decimal to the left).

#### Worked Example: Converting to Scientific Notation
Let's convert the number \( 45,000 \) into scientific notation.

1. Move the decimal point so that the coefficient is between 1 and 10. For \( 45,000 \), the decimal is currently at the end. We move it 4 places to the left to get \( 4.5 \).
2. Since we moved the decimal 4 places to the left, our exponent for 10 will be \( +4 \).
3. The result is:
   \[ 4.5 \times 10^4 \]

This notation allows us to represent \( 45,000 \) succinctly and provides a clear view of the number's magnitude.

## Solving Multi-Step Equations

To find the value of a variable that is part of a more complex relationship, we must solve **multi-step equations**. Unlike one-step or two-step equations, multi-step equations require a sequence of operations to isolate the variable.

The goal is always the same: perform inverse operations to get the variable by itself on one side of the equation. This typically involves:
1. Simplifying both sides of the equation (using the distributive property and combining like terms).
2. Moving all variable terms to one side and all constant terms to the other.
3. Isolating the variable using multiplication or division.

#### Worked Example: Solving a Multi-Step Equation
Let's solve the equation \( 2(x - 3) + 4 = 10 \).

1. **Distribute** the \( 2 \) into the parentheses:
   \[ 2x - 6 + 4 = 10 \]
2. **Combine like terms** on the left side (\(-6 + 4\)):
   \[ 2x - 2 = 10 \]
3. **Add 2** to both sides to isolate the \( 2x \) term:
   \[ 2x = 12 \]
4. **Divide by 2** on both sides to isolate \( x \):
   \[ x = 6 \]

By following these steps systematically, we can unravel even complex algebraic relationships.

## Literal Equations

In many scientific and engineering applications, we work with **literal equations**. A literal equation is an equation that contains two or more variables, where one variable is being solved for in terms of the others. Instead of finding a specific numerical value, we find a formula.

Common examples include the formula for area (\( A = lw \)) or the ideal gas law (\( PV = nRT \)). In these cases, we treat the other variables as if they were constants to isolate the target variable.

#### Worked Example: Solving for a Variable
Let's solve the formula for \( w \) in the equation \( A = lw \).

1. The goal is to isolate \( w \). Currently, \( w \) is being multiplied by \( l \).
2. Perform the **inverse operation** by dividing both sides of the equation by \( l \):
   \[ \frac{A}{l} = \frac{lw}{l} \]
3. Simplify the right side:
   \[ w = \frac{A}{l} \]

We have now expressed \( w \) as a function of \( A \) and \( l \). This formula can be used to find the width of any rectangle if the area and length are known.

## Subtracting Polynomials

A **polynomial** is an expression consisting of variables and coefficients. When we perform operations on them, we must be careful with signs, especially when **subtracting polynomials**.

Subtracting one polynomial from another is essentially the same as adding the *opposite* of the polynomial being subtracted. This is because the subtraction sign must be distributed to every single term within the polynomial that follows it.

For example, to subtract \( (x - 5) \) from \( (3x + 2) \), we rewrite it as \( (3x + 2) - (x - 5) \), which becomes \( (3x + 2) + (-x + 5) \).

## Difference of Squares

The **difference of squares** is a special pattern in factoring that occurs when we have a binomial where both terms are perfect squares and they are being subtracted.

The pattern is expressed as:
\[ a^2 - b^2 = (a - b)(a + b) \]

Recognizing this pattern allows for rapid factoring without the need for more laborious methods like trinomial factoring or the quadratic formula.

#### Worked Example: Factoring a Difference of Squares
Let's factor the expression \( x^2 - 49 \).

1. Identify the two terms: The terms are \( x^2 \) and \( 49 \).
2. Check if they are perfect squares:
   - \( x^2 \) is the square of \( x \).
   - \( 49 \) is the square of \( 7 \).
3. Apply the pattern \( (a - b)(a + b) \), where \( a = x \) and \( b = 7 \):
   \[ x^2 - 49 = (x - 7)(x + 7) \]

This factored form reveals the roots of the expression and is a key step in solving higher-order equations.

## Function Domain

When we talk about a function, we are describing a relationship between two sets of values. However, not every input value is necessarily valid for every function. The **domain** of a function is the complete set of all possible starting values (inputs) for which the function is defined and produces a real number.

In algebra, finding the domain often involves identifying values that would cause mathematical errors. For example, we cannot divide by zero, and we cannot take the square root of a negative number (within the realm of real numbers). Therefore, the domain is restricted to exclude any input that would violate these rules.

#### Worked Example: Finding the Domain
Let's find the domain of the function \( f(x) = \frac{5}{x - 3} \).

1. Identify potential restrictions: The function has a denominator, \( x - 3 \).
2. Determine which value makes the denominator zero:
   \[ x - 3 = 0 \implies x = 3 \]
3. State the domain: Since \( x = 3 \) would cause division by zero, the domain is all real numbers except \( 3 \).

In interval notation, this is written as \( (-\infty, 3) \cup (3, \infty) \).

## Vertex Form of Quadratics

While many quadratic functions are written in standard form (\( ax^2 + bx + c \)), there is another highly useful representation called **vertex form**. The vertex form explicitly shows the coordinates of the parabola's highest or lowest point, known as the vertex.

The vertex form of a quadratic function is written as:
\[ f(x) = a(x - h)^2 + k \]

In this form:
- \( (h, k) \) is the coordinates of the vertex.
- \( a \) determines the steepness of the parabola and whether it opens upward (\( a > 0 \)) or downward (\( a < 0 \)).

Using vertex form is particularly helpful for graphing and for understanding the transformations applied to the parent function \( f(x) = x^2 \).

#### Worked Example: Identifying the Vertex
Let's find the vertex of the quadratic function \( f(x) = 2(x - 4)^2 + 7 \).

1. Compare the given function to the vertex form \( f(x) = a(x - h)^2 + k \).
2. Identify the values of \( h \) and \( k \):
   - \( h = 4 \)
   - \( k = 7 \)
3. The vertex is \( (4, 7) \).

Because \( a = 2 \) is positive, we also know that this parabola opens upward, and the vertex \( (4, 7) \) represents the minimum point of the function.

## Rate of Change

One of the most important concepts in algebra and calculus is the **rate of change**. This concept describes how one quantity changes in relation to another. In the context of linear functions, the rate of change is constant and is known as the **slope**.

The slope represents the "steepness" of a line. It tells us how much the output value (usually \( y \)) changes for every one-unit increase in the input value (usually \( x \)). A positive slope means the function is increasing, a negative slope means it is decreasing, and a zero slope means the function is constant.

#### Worked Example: Calculating Slope
Let's find the rate of change (slope) for a line that passes through the points \( (2, 5) \) and \( (4, 13) \).

1. Use the slope formula:
   \[ m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} \]
2. Substitute the coordinates of the points:
   \[ m = \frac{13 - 5}{4 - 2} \]
3. Simplify the expression:
   \[ m = \frac{8}{2} = 4 \]

The rate of change is \( 4 \), meaning that for every one unit increase in \( x \), the value of \( y \) increases by 4 units.

## Scatterplots

When we collect data from real-world observations, we often use **scatterplots** to visualize the relationship between two variables. A scatterplot is a graph in which the values of two variables are plotted as individual points on a coordinate plane.

By looking at the pattern formed by these points, we can identify whether there is a correlation between the variables. For instance, if the points generally trend upward from left to right, there is a positive correlation. If they trend downward, there is a negative correlation. If they appear random, there is no correlation.

#### Worked Example: Interpreting a Scatterplot
Imagine a scatterplot where the horizontal axis (\( x \)) represents the hours spent studying and the vertical axis (\( y \)) represents the score on a test.

1. Observe the trend: If the points form a pattern that moves upward and to the right, it indicates a **positive correlation**.
2. Interpret the meaning: This suggests that as the number of hours spent studying increases, the test scores also tend to increase.
3. Conclude: A student can use this visualization to conclude that studying is generally associated with higher performance on this particular test.

## Mathematical Modeling

**Mathematical modeling** is the process of using mathematical language to describe and predict real-world phenomena. It involves translating a real-world situation into a mathematical equation or function, solving that equation, and then interpreting the results back in the context of the original problem.

Modeling is a iterative process that often involves several stages:
1. **Identification:** Defining the variables and the relationships between them.
2. **Formulation:** Creating a mathematical model (e.g., a linear or exponential function).
3. **Solution:** Using algebraic techniques to solve the model.
4. **Interpretation:** Translating the mathematical solution back into real-world terms.

#### Worked Example: Modeling a Linear Relationship
A taxi company charges a flat fee of \( \$5 \) plus \( \$2 \) for every mile driven. We want to create a model to predict the total cost of a ride.

1. **Identify variables:** Let \( C \) be the total cost and \( m \) be the number of miles driven.
2. **Formulate the model:** The cost consists of a constant (\( \$5 \)) and a variable part (\( \$2 \) per mile).
   \[ C = 2m + 5 \]
3. **Use the model:** If a passenger travels \( 10 \) miles, we can predict the cost:
   \[ C = 2(10) + 5 = 25 \]
4. **Interpret:** The predicted cost for a 10-mile ride is \( \$25 \).

This model allows the taxi company and its customers to make informed decisions based on predicted costs.
