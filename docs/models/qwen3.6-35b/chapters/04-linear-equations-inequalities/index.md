## 4.1 Simplifying Numerical Expressions Before You Solve

Before you can solve any equation, you need to be comfortable simplifying numerical expressions that appear on either side of the equals sign. Many students stumble not because they do not understand equations — they stumble because the expression in front of them has not been simplified yet.

Recall the **order of operations** (often remembered by PEMDAS or BODMAS): Parentheses, Exponents, Multiplication and Division (left to right), Addition and Subtraction (left to right). Always follow this order when simplifying a numerical expression.

### Worked Example 4.1

Simplify the expression on the left side:

`6 + 2 · (9 − 4) + 3²`

The steps, in order, are:

1. **Parentheses:** `9 − 4 = 5`. The expression becomes `6 + 2 · 5 + 3²`.
2. **Exponents:** `3² = 9`. The expression becomes `6 + 2 · 5 + 9`.
3. **Multiplication:** `2 · 5 = 10`. The expression becomes `6 + 10 + 9`.
4. **Addition:** `6 + 10 + 9 = 25`.

**Final answer:** The simplified expression equals 25.

Before we move to equations, notice a key skill inside worked example 4.1: the multiplication (`2 · 5`) had to be done before the addition (`6 +`). That is not optional — it is dictated by the order of operations. Whenever you see an expression with multiple operations, slow down and mark the order in which you will perform them.

### Properties You Will Use Again

Below are the properties from Chapter 1 that serve as your toolkit for solving equations. Each one tells you when two expressions are guaranteed to be equal even though they look different.

| Property | Statement | Example |
|---------|-----------|---------|
| **Commutative (Addition)** | `a + b = b + a` | `3 + 7 = 7 + 3` |
| **Associative (Addition)** | `(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)` | `(2 + 3) + 4 = 2 + (3 + 4)` |
| **Commutative (Multiplication)** | `a · b = b · a` | `5 · 3 = 3 · 5` |
| **Associative (Multiplication)** | `(a · b) · c = a · (b · c)` | `(2 · 3) · 4 = 2 · (3 · 4)` |
| **Distributive** | `a(b + c) = ab + ac` | `3(x + 4) = 3x + 12` |

The distributive property is especially important. Whenever you encounter a number (or variable) directly in front of parentheses, you must multiply that number by every term inside the parentheses. Forgetting to distribute to all terms is one of the most common algebra mistakes.

## 4.2 One-Step Equations

A **one-step equation** is an equation that requires exactly one inverse operation to isolate the variable. The four basic operations give you four types: addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division.

The guiding principle in every step of solving an equation is this: **whatever you do to one side of the equals sign, you must do to the other side**. The equality is a balance — tip one side and the whole equation breaks.

### Solving Equations That Involve Addition or Subtraction

If the variable has a number added (or subtracted) to it, undo that operation by subtracting (or adding) the same number from both sides.

### Worked Example 4.2

Solve `x + 9 = −3`.

The variable `x` has 9 added to it. Undo by subtracting 9 from both sides:

`x + 9 − 9 = −3 − 9`
`x = −12`

Check by substituting back: `−12 + 9 = −3`. Correct.

### Worked Example 4.3

Solve `y − 7 = 11`.

The variable `y` has 7 subtracted from it. Undo by adding 7 to both sides:

`y − 7 + 7 = 11 + 7`
`y = 18`

Check: `18 − 7 = 11`. Correct.

### Solving Equations That Involve Multiplication or Division

When the variable is multiplied by a number, divide both sides by that number. When it is divided by a number, multiply both sides by that number. The inverse relationship of multiplication and division lets you undo each operation.

### Worked Example 4.4

Solve `5x = 35`.

`x` is multiplied by 5. Divide both sides by 5:

`(5x) / 5 = 35 / 5`
`x = 7`

Check: `5 · 7 = 35`. Correct.

### Worked Example 4.5

Solve `y / 4 = −6`.

`y` is divided by 4. Multiply both sides by 4:

`(y / 4) · 4 = −6 · 4`
`y = −24`

Check: `(−24) / 4 = −6`. Correct.

## 4.3 Two-Step Equations

A **two-step equation** requires two inverse operations to isolate the variable. These equations typically take the form `ax + b = c` or `b − ax = c`. The general strategy is:

1. Undo addition or subtraction first (get the term with the variable by itself).
2. Undo multiplication or division second (isolate the variable completely).

The order matters. If you try to divide before subtracting, the arithmetic becomes unnecessarily complicated and increases the chance of an error.

### Worked Example 4.6

Solve `3x + 7 = −8`.

- Step 1: Subtract 7 from both sides to move the constant term to the right.
  `3x + 7 − 7 = −8 − 7`
  `3x = −15`

- Step 2: Divide both sides by 3 (the coefficient of x).
  `(3x) / 3 = (−15) / 3`
  `x = −5`

Check: `3(−5) + 7 = −15 + 7 = −8`. Correct.

### Worked Example 4.7

Solve `10 − 2y = −4`.

- Step 1: Subtract 10 from both sides to isolate the variable term.
  `10 − 10 − 2y = −4 − 10`
  `−2y = −14`

- Step 2: Divide both sides by −2.
  `(−2y) / (−2) = (−14) / (−2)`
  `y = 7`

Check: `10 − 2(7) = 10 − 14 = −4`. Correct.

Notice in worked example 4.7 that dividing two negatives gives a positive — `−14 / −2 = 7`. This is consistent with the rule from Chapter 1 that an even number of negative signs in a product or quotient yields a positive result.

### Worked Example 4.8

Solve `(x / 3) + 4 = 10`.

- Step 1: Subtract 4 from both sides.
  `x / 3 + 4 − 4 = 10 − 4`
  `x / 3 = 6`

- Step 2: Multiply both sides by 3.
  `(x / 3) · 3 = 6 · 3`
  `x = 18`

Check: `18 / 3 + 4 = 6 + 4 = 10`. Correct.

## 4.4 Multi-Step Equations

A **multi-step equation** requires three or more operations. These equations may have variables on both sides, require the distributive property, or contain fractions and decimals. The same strategy applies — isolate the variable by undoing operations one at a time — but there are more moves to plan ahead.

### Strategy for Multi-Step Equations

Before you start manipulating numbers, do this:

1. **Simplify each side separately.** Use the distributive property to expand any parentheses. Combine like terms (terms with the same variable raised to the same power) on each side of the equals sign.
2. **Move all variable terms to one side** using addition or subtraction.
3. **Move all constant terms to the other side** using addition or subtraction.
4. **Isolate the variable** using multiplication or division.
5. **Check your answer** by substitution.

### Worked Example 4.9

Solve `4(x − 3) + 2 = 3x + 7`.

- Step 1: Distribute on the left side, then simplify.
  `4x − 12 + 2 = 3x + 7`
  `4x − 10 = 3x + 7`

- Step 2: Move variable terms to one side. Subtract 3x from both sides.
  `4x − 3x − 10 = 7`
  `x − 10 = 7`

- Step 3: Isolate x by adding 10 to both sides.
  `x − 10 + 10 = 7 + 10`
  `x = 17`

Check: Left side: `4(17 − 3) + 2 = 4(14) + 2 = 56 + 2 = 58`. Right side: `3(17) + 7 = 51 + 7 = 58`. Both sides match.

### Worked Example 4.10

Solve `5 − 2(x + 3) = 3x − (x − 8)`.

- Step 1: Distribute on both sides.
  Left side: `5 − 2x − 6`
  Right side: `3x − x + 8` (note the sign change when distributing the minus sign!)
  `−2x − 1 = 2x + 8`

- Step 2: Move all variable terms to one side. Add 2x to both sides.
  `−1 = 4x + 8`

- Step 3: Move constants to the other side. Subtract 8 from both sides.
  `−9 = 4x`

- Step 4: Isolate x. Divide by 4.
  `x = −9/4` or `x = −2¼`

Check: Left side: `5 − 2(−9/4 + 3) = 5 − 2(3/4) = 5 − 3/2 = 10/2 − 3/2 = 7/2`. Right side: `3(−9/4) − (−9/4 − 8) = −27/4 + 9/4 + 32/4 = 14/4 = 7/2`. Both sides match.

The distributive property in worked example 4.10 is especially important on the right side. The minus sign before `(x − 8)` means you must change the sign of **every** term inside the parentheses: `−(x − 8) = −x + 8`. This is a common source of errors because it is easy to forget the second sign flip.

## 4.5 Literal Equations and Formulas

A **literal equation** (also called a **formula**) is an equation that contains two or more variables — for example, `d = rt` (distance equals rate times time) or `A = lw` (area of a rectangle equals length times width). Solving a literal equation means isolating one variable in terms of the others. The process is identical to solving numerical equations: treat the variable you want as the unknown and every other letter as if it were a constant.

### Worked Example 4.11

Solve `A = lw` for `w`.

The variable `w` is multiplied by `l`. Divide both sides by `l`:

`A / l = (lw) / l`
`A / l = w` or `w = A / l`

This tells you that the width of a rectangle equals its area divided by its length — a useful rearrangement when you know the area and one side.

### Worked Example 4.12

Solve `d = rt` for `t`.

`t` is multiplied by `r`. Divide both sides by `r`:

`d / r = (rt) / r`
`t = d / r`

This rearranged formula says: time equals distance divided by rate. It is the basis of many word problems involving speed and travel.

### Worked Example 4.13

Solve `3x + 2y = 6` for `y`.

This equation has both `x` and `y`. We want to isolate `y` as if `x` were just a number.

- Step 1: Subtract `3x` from both sides.
  `2y = 6 − 3x`

- Step 2: Divide both sides by 2.
  `y = (6 − 3x) / 2` or `y = 3 − (3/2)x`

The expression on the right is now written entirely in terms of `x`. This form (`y = mx + b`) is called **slope-intercept form**, and we will explore it in more depth in Chapter 5. For now, notice that you can solve for any variable by treating the others as known numbers.

## 4.6 Solving Inequalities

An **inequality** is similar to an equation, but instead of an equals sign it uses `<`, `>`, `≤`, or `≥`. The key idea is the same: you perform inverse operations to isolate the variable, and whatever you do to one side you must do to the other.

### The One Big Difference

There is one critical rule that applies to inequalities but **not** to equations:

> **If you multiply or divide both sides of an inequality by a negative number, you must flip (reverse) the direction of the inequality sign.**

This rule exists because multiplying or dividing by a negative reverses the order of numbers on the number line. For example, `3 < 5` is true, but if you multiply both sides by −1 without flipping the sign you would get `−3 < −5`, which is false (because −3 is actually greater than −5). Flip the sign instead: `−3 > −5`. Now it is correct.

### Worked Example 4.14

Solve and graph `2x + 3 > 11`.

- Step 1: Subtract 3 from both sides.
  `2x > 8`

- Step 2: Divide by 2 (positive, so no flip).
  `x > 4`

On a number line, this is shown as an open circle at 4 with shading to the right — all numbers greater than 4 but not including 4 itself.

### Worked Example 4.15

Solve and graph `−3x − 7 ≤ 8`.

- Step 1: Add 7 to both sides.
  `−3x ≤ 15`

- Step 2: Divide by −3 (negative, so **flip** the sign).
  `x ≥ −5`

On a number line, this is shown as a closed circle at −5 with shading to the right — all numbers greater than or equal to −5.

### Worked Example 4.16

Solve and graph `5 − 2x < −3`.

- Step 1: Subtract 5 from both sides.
  `−2x < −8`

- Step 2: Divide by −2 (negative, so **flip** the sign).
  `x > 4`

On a number line, this is an open circle at 4 with shading to the right.

## 4.7 Compound Inequalities

A **compound inequality** is formed by joining two simple inequalities with "and" or "or." There are two types:

- **And compound inequalities** — both conditions must be true simultaneously (intersection). The solution lies in the overlap of both individual solutions.
- **Or compound inequalities** — either condition can be true (union). The solution includes all values that satisfy at least one side.

### And Compound Inequalities

When an inequality is written as `a < x < b`, it means "x is greater than a AND x is less than b." Solve these by performing the same operation on all three parts simultaneously — just like an equation, but with three pieces instead of two.

### Worked Example 4.17

Solve `−3 < 2x + 1 ≤ 9`.

Perform operations on all three parts:

- Subtract 1 from each part: `−3 − 1 < 2x + 1 − 1 ≤ 9 − 1`, which gives `−4 < 2x ≤ 8`.
- Divide each part by 2 (positive, no flip): `−4 / 2 < x ≤ 8 / 2`, which gives `−2 < x ≤ 4`.

On a number line: an open circle at −2, a closed circle at 4, and the region between shaded. The solution is all real numbers strictly greater than −2 and less than or equal to 4.

### Or Compound Inequalities

For `a < x` OR `x < b`, solve each inequality independently and combine the results as a union.

### Worked Example 4.18

Solve `3x − 2 < 7` OR `3x − 2 > 10`.

Left side: `3x < 9` → `x < 3`.
Right side: `3x > 12` → `x > 4`.

The solution is `x < 3` or `x > 4`. On a number line, there are two shaded regions: everything to the left of 3 (open circle) and everything to the right of 4 (open circle), with a gap between them.

## 4.8 Absolute Value Equations

The **absolute value** of a number, written `|n|`, represents its distance from zero on the number line — regardless of direction. Because distance is always non-negative, `|5| = 5` and `|−5| = 5`. This property creates an important consequence for equations: when you have `|expression| = k` where `k > 0`, the expression inside can equal either `+k` or `−k`.

### Solving Absolute Value Equations — The Two-Case Method

For any equation of the form `|A| = k` (where `k ≥ 0`), write two separate equations:

`A = k` OR `A = −k`

Solve each one independently. If `k < 0`, there is no solution because absolute value can never be negative.

### Worked Example 4.19

Solve `|2x − 3| = 7`.

Set up the two cases:

Case 1: `2x − 3 = 7`
`2x = 10`
`x = 5`

Case 2: `2x − 3 = −7`
`2x = −4`
`x = −2`

Check: `|2(5) − 3| = |7| = 7`. ✓ `|2(−2) − 3| = |−7| = 7`. ✓

**Solutions:** `x = 5` or `x = −2`.

### Worked Example 4.20

Solve `|x + 4| = 3`.

Case 1: `x + 4 = 3` → `x = −1`.
Case 2: `x + 4 = −3` → `x = −7`.

Check: `|−1 + 4| = |3| = 3`. ✓ `|−7 + 4| = |−3| = 3`. ✓

**Solutions:** `x = −1` or `x = −7`.

### Worked Example 4.21

Solve `|3x + 1| + 5 = 5`.

First, isolate the absolute value expression:
`|3x + 1| = 0`

When an absolute value equals zero, there is only one case (since `+0 = −0`):
`3x + 1 = 0`
`3x = −1`
`x = −1/3`

Check: `|3(−1/3) + 1| + 5 = |0| + 5 = 0 + 5 = 5`. ✓

**Solution:** `x = −1/3`. (Only one solution because the absolute value equals zero, not a positive number.)

### Number Line Visualization of Absolute Value Solutions

Absolute value equations always have two solutions on the number line when `k > 0`, because the expression inside is equally distant from zero in both directions. The solutions are symmetric around the point where the inside expression equals zero (the "center" of the absolute value).

## 4.9 Absolute Value Inequalities

Absolute value inequalities extend the two-case method. There are two forms, and they lead to different types of compound inequalities:

| Form | Meaning on Number Line | Compound Inequality |
|------|------------------------|---------------------|
| `|A| < k` (where `k > 0`) | A is within distance k of zero — between −k and +k | `−k < A < k` (an **and** inequality) |
| `|A| > k` (where `k > 0`) | A is farther than k from zero — less than −k or greater than +k | `A < −k` **or** `A > k` (an **or** inequality) |

For `≤` and `≥`, replace `<` with `≤` and `>` with `≥` accordingly.

### Worked Example 4.22

Solve `|x − 2| ≤ 5`.

The form is `|A| ≤ k`, so rewrite as an **and** inequality:
`−5 ≤ x − 2 ≤ 5`

Add 2 to all three parts:
`−3 ≤ x ≤ 7`

On a number line: closed circles at −3 and 7, with the region between shaded. The solution is all real numbers from −3 to 7, inclusive.

### Worked Example 4.23

Solve `|2x + 1| > 7`.

The form is `|A| > k`, so rewrite as an **or** inequality:
`2x + 1 < −7` **OR** `2x + 1 > 7`

Solve each side independently:
Left: `2x < −8` → `x < −4`.
Right: `2x > 6` → `x > 3`.

On a number line: open circles at −4 and 3, with the region to the left of −4 and the region to the right of 3 both shaded. There is a gap between them (the numbers that do NOT satisfy the inequality).

### Worked Example 4.24

Solve `|3x − 6| + 2 ≥ 11`.

First isolate the absolute value:
`|3x − 6| ≥ 9`

Rewrite as an **or** inequality:
`3x − 6 ≤ −9` **OR** `3x − 6 ≥ 9`

Left: `3x ≤ −3` → `x ≤ −1`.
Right: `3x ≥ 15` → `x ≥ 5`.

**Solutions:** `x ≤ −1` or `x ≥ 5`.

### Worked Example 4.25

Solve `|x/2 + 1| < 3`.

Rewrite as an **and** inequality:
`−3 < x/2 + 1 < 3`

Subtract 1 from all three parts:
`−4 < x/2 < 2`

Multiply all three parts by 2 (positive, no flip):
`−8 < x < 4`

**Solution:** All real numbers strictly between −8 and 4.

## 4.10 Systems of Linear Equations

A **system of linear equations** consists of two or more linear equations with the same variables. A solution to the system is a set of values for the variables that satisfies **every** equation simultaneously. Geometrically, each equation represents a line in the coordinate plane, and the solution corresponds to the point where the lines intersect.

There are three standard methods for solving systems of two equations:

1. **Graphing** — find the intersection point visually
2. **Substitution** — solve one equation for a variable and substitute into the other
3. **Elimination (addition)** — add or subtract the equations to eliminate one variable

### Method 1: Graphing Systems

When you graph two lines, three outcomes are possible:

- **One solution:** The lines intersect at exactly one point. This happens when the lines have different slopes.
- **No solution:** The lines are parallel (same slope, different y-intercept). They never meet.
- **Infinitely many solutions:** The lines are identical (same slope and same intercept). Every point on the line is a solution.

To use graphing, convert both equations to slope-intercept form (`y = mx + b`), plot each line using its slope and y-intercept, and read the intersection point. While this method gives good intuition, it is only approximate — for exact answers, use substitution or elimination.

### Method 2: Substitution Method

The **substitution method** works well when one of the equations already has a variable isolated (or can be easily isolated). Follow these steps:

1. Solve one equation for one variable (preferably the easier one).
2. Substitute that expression into the other equation — wherever the variable appears, replace it with the expression you just found.
3. Solve the resulting single-variable equation.
4. Substitute the answer back into one of the original equations to find the other variable.
5. Check both values in both original equations.

### Worked Example 4.26 — Substitution Method

Solve: `y = 2x + 3` and `3x + y = −8`.

- Step 1: The first equation already has `y` isolated: `y = 2x + 3`.
- Step 2: Substitute `2x + 3` for `y` in the second equation.
  `3x + (2x + 3) = −8`
- Step 3: Solve for x.
  `5x + 3 = −8`
  `5x = −11`
  `x = −11/5` or `x = −2.2`
- Step 4: Substitute `x = −11/5` back into `y = 2x + 3`.
  `y = 2(−11/5) + 3 = −22/5 + 15/5 = −7/5` or `y = −1.4`

Solution: `x = −11/5`, `y = −7/5`. (Check by substituting into both original equations.)

### Worked Example 4.27 — Substitution Method

Solve: `2x + y = 10` and `x − y = 2`.

- Step 1: Solve the second equation for `y`.
  `x − 2 = y` or `y = x − 2`
- Step 2: Substitute into the first equation.
  `2x + (x − 2) = 10`
  `3x − 2 = 10`
  `3x = 12`
  `x = 4`
- Step 3: Substitute back to find y.
  `y = 4 − 2 = 2`

Solution: `(4, 2)`. Check: `2(4) + 2 = 10` ✓ and `4 − 2 = 2` ✓.

### Method 3: Elimination Method

The **elimination method** (also called the addition method) works by adding or subtracting the two equations to cancel out one variable. Follow these steps:

1. Write both equations in standard form (`Ax + By = C`).
2. If necessary, multiply one or both equations by a constant so that the coefficients of one variable become opposites (same magnitude, opposite sign).
3. Add the two equations — the chosen variable cancels out.
4. Solve the resulting single-variable equation.
5. Substitute back to find the other variable.
6. Check in both original equations.

### Worked Example 4.28 — Elimination Method

Solve: `3x + 2y = 12` and `x − 2y = 4`.

Notice that the coefficients of `y` are already opposites (+2 and −2). Add the equations directly:

`(3x + x) + (2y − 2y) = 12 + 4`
`4x + 0 = 16`
`4x = 16`
`x = 4`

Substitute `x = 4` into either equation. Using the second: `4 − 2y = 4`, so `−2y = 0`, and `y = 0`.

Solution: `(4, 0)`. Check: `3(4) + 2(0) = 12` ✓ and `4 − 2(0) = 4` ✓.

### Worked Example 4.29 — Elimination Method (Requires Multiplication First)

Solve: `2x + 3y = 12` and `4x − y = 6`.

The coefficients of neither variable are already opposites. Let's eliminate `y`. Multiply the second equation by 3 so its `y` coefficient becomes −3 (the opposite of +3):

First equation: `2x + 3y = 12`
Second equation × 3: `12x − 3y = 18`

Add:
`(2x + 12x) + (3y − 3y) = 12 + 18`
`14x = 30`
`x = 30/14 = 15/7` or `x ≈ 2.14`

Substitute into the second original equation: `4(15/7) − y = 6`.
`60/7 − y = 6 = 42/7`
`y = 60/7 − 42/7 = 18/7` or `y ≈ 2.57`

Solution: `(15/7, 18/7)`. Check in both equations confirms correctness.

## 4.11 Systems of Inequalities

A **system of inequalities** is a set of two or more inequalities with the same variables. The solution to a system of inequalities is the region (or regions) in the coordinate plane where **all** inequalities overlap — the area that satisfies every inequality simultaneously.

To graph a system of inequalities:

1. Graph each inequality on the same coordinate plane exactly as you did in section 4.6.
2. Shade the solution region for each inequality separately (you can use different line styles or colors).
3. The **solution to the system** is the region where all shaded areas overlap — the darkened region common to every inequality.

### Worked Example 4.30

Graph and describe the solution of:

`y > x + 1` and `y < −x + 4`

- First inequality (`y > x + 1`): Draw the line `y = x + 1` (dashed, because `>` does not include the boundary) and shade above the line.
- Second inequality (`y < −x + 4`): Draw the line `y = −x + 4` (dashed) and shade below the line.
- The solution is the triangular region where both shadings overlap — between the two lines, above `y = x + 1` and below `y = −x + 4`.

To verify a point is in the solution region, substitute it into **both** inequalities. For example, `(0, 2)`: `2 > 0 + 1` (true) and `2 < −0 + 4` (true), so `(0, 2)` is a valid solution.

### Worked Example 4.31

Graph and describe the solution of:

`y ≥ 2x − 3` and `y ≤ −x + 6`

- First inequality (`y ≥ 2x − 3`): Solid line, shade above.
- Second inequality (`y ≤ −x + 6`): Solid line, shade below.
- The solution is the region where the shadings overlap — a wedge between the two lines.

Find the intersection point of the boundary lines to locate the vertex of the wedge:
Set `2x − 3 = −x + 6`.
`3x = 9` → `x = 3`.
Substitute: `y = 2(3) − 3 = 3`.

The vertex is at `(3, 3)`, and the solution region extends to the left from this point, bounded above by `y = −x + 6` and below by `y = 2x − 3`.

## 4.12 Connecting to Quadratic Expressions

Although this chapter focuses on linear equations, several topics here serve as prerequisites for solving quadratic equations — a central theme of the next chapters. Understanding how simplification, factoring, and completing the square work with linear expressions builds the foundation you need.

### Greatest Common Factor (GCF) Review

When you factor a polynomial, the first step is always to look for the **greatest common factor** shared by all terms. The GCF of a set of terms consists of:

- The greatest integer that divides every coefficient evenly
- Every variable that appears in **every** term, raised to the lowest power it appears at in any term

### Worked Example 4.32

Factor `6x³ + 9x² − 12x` completely.

Step 1: Find the GCF of all three terms.
- Coefficients: GCF(6, 9, 12) = 3.
- Variables: only x appears in every term; lowest power is x¹.
- GCF = `3x`.

Step 2: Divide each term by the GCF and write as a product:
`3x(2x² + 3x − 4)`

The expression is factored as `3x(2x² + 3x − 4)`. The quadratic inside can be checked for further factoring (it does not factor nicely over the integers, so we stop here).

### Trinomial Factoring Review

To factor a trinomial of the form `ax² + bx + c`:

1. Find two numbers whose product is `a · c` and whose sum is `b`.
2. Rewrite the middle term using these two numbers (this creates four terms).
3. Factor by grouping: factor the GCF from pairs of terms, then factor out the common binomial.

When `a = 1` (the trinomial starts with `x²`), this simplifies: find two numbers whose product is `c` and whose sum is `b`.

### Worked Example 4.33 — Trinomial Factoring (a = 1)

Factor `x² + 5x + 6`.

Find two numbers whose product is 6 and whose sum is 5. The pairs of factors of 6 are: (1, 6), (2, 3). Only (2, 3) adds to 5.

`x² + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3)`

Check by expanding: `(x + 2)(x + 3) = x² + 3x + 2x + 6 = x² + 5x + 6`. ✓

### Prime Polynomials

A polynomial is **prime** (or irreducible over the integers) when it cannot be factored into two polynomials with integer coefficients. In other words, no matter what factoring strategies you try — GCF, grouping, trinomial formulas — there are no two integer-coefficient factors that multiply to produce the original expression.

### Worked Example 4.34

Determine whether `x² + x + 1` is prime.

To factor `x² + x + 1`, look for two integers whose product is 1 and whose sum is 1. The only factor pairs of 1 are (1, 1) and (−1, −1), which give sums of 2 and −2 respectively. Neither gives a sum of 1.

No other strategies apply (no GCF beyond 1, not a special product pattern).

**Conclusion:** `x² + x + 1` is prime over the integers.

### Completing the Square: The Bridge to Quadratics

**Completing the square** is a technique that rewrites a quadratic expression in the form `(x − h)² + k`. This technique was introduced briefly in Chapter 3 and will be explored deeply when you study quadratic equations. The key idea is:

Given `x² + bx`, add `(b/2)²` to create a perfect square trinomial:
`x² + bx + (b/2)² = (x + b/2)²`

### Worked Example 4.35 — Completing the Square

Rewrite `x² + 8x + 3` in vertex form `(x − h)² + k`.

- The coefficient of x is 8, so `b/2 = 4` and `(b/2)² = 16`.
- Add and subtract 16 (adding zero doesn't change the expression):
  `x² + 8x + 16 − 16 + 3`
  `(x + 4)² − 13`

The vertex form is `(x + 4)² − 13`, which reveals that the parabola `y = x² + 8x + 3` has its vertex at `(−4, −13)`. The number 13 also appears in the next section on the discriminant — when you set the expression equal to zero and solve, the roots lie exactly 13 units below the horizontal shift.

### Roots of Quadratics via Completing the Square

If we set `x² + 8x + 3 = 0` and complete the square:

`(x + 4)² − 13 = 0`
`(x + 4)² = 13`
`x + 4 = ±√13`
`x = −4 ± √13`

**Solutions:** `x = −4 + √13` and `x = −4 − √13`. (These are approximately `0.61` and `−8.61`.)

### The Discriminant Preview

The **discriminant** of a quadratic equation `ax² + bx + c = 0` is the expression `b² − 4ac`. Its value determines the nature of the roots:

| Discriminant Value | Nature of Roots |
|--------------------|-----------------|
| `b² − 4ac > 0` | Two distinct real roots (the quadratic crosses the x-axis at two points) |
| `b² − 4ac = 0` | One repeated real root (the vertex touches the x-axis — a perfect square trinomial) |
| `b² − 4ac < 0` | No real roots (the parabola does not intersect the x-axis — roots are complex) |

In worked example 4.35, for `x² + 8x + 3`: `a = 1`, `b = 8`, `c = 3`.
Discriminant: `8² − 4(1)(3) = 64 − 12 = 52 > 0`. Two distinct real roots — consistent with the ±√13 result.

## Chapter Summary

This chapter covered a wide range of algebraic techniques, all connected by one central idea: **isolate the unknown**. Whether you are solving an equation or an inequality, the goal is always the same — use inverse operations to get the variable alone on one side.

### Key Takeaways

1. **Simplify first.** Always apply the distributive property and combine like terms before isolating variables.
2. **Balance is sacred.** Whatever operation you perform on one side, perform it on the other. This is the golden rule of equation solving.
3. **One-step → two-step → multi-step.** Build fluency with simpler forms before tackling complex expressions. Practice each type until you can solve it without hesitation.
4. **Literal equations** use the same methods — just treat other letters as known numbers.
5. **Inequalities follow all equation rules plus one more:** flip the sign when multiplying or dividing by a negative. This is non-negotiable.
6. **Absolute value equations split into two cases.** When you see `|expression| = k`, write both `expression = k` and `expression = −k`.
7. **Systems of equations** can be solved by graphing (for intuition), substitution (when one variable is isolated), or elimination (when coefficients line up nicely).
8. **Systems of inequalities** are solved by finding the overlapping shaded region — every inequality must be satisfied simultaneously.
9. **Factoring, completing the square, and the discriminant** all build on the algebraic manipulation skills you practiced in this chapter. They will become central tools when you study quadratic equations.

### Looking Ahead

In the next chapters, you will extend everything you have learned here: solving quadratic equations (by factoring, square roots, completing the square, and the quadratic formula), exploring exponential growth and decay, analyzing sequences, and building mathematical models for real-world situations. The isolation technique — undoing operations one by one to find the unknown — is the thread that ties all of these topics together.
