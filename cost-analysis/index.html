    Cost Analysis¶

    This page estimates what a textbook costs when one DGX system generates it. It adds up the cost to buy the system, set it up, and run it, and spreads that cost over the books the system can produce. It then works out what a book-generation service would have to charge to keep a 20% profit margin.

    Under the assumptions below, one unit costs about $8,300 a year to own and run. With the reference model, a whole textbook takes about 28 hours of machine time and costs about $53, so the service would have to charge about $66. Across the ten candidate models the price runs from about $40 to about $216.

    An estimate built on one measured run

    Only one model has finished one benchmark stage, and no model has generated a whole book on this hardware. Nobody has judged the quality of any output. Every time on this page except that one stage is calculated from assumptions. Treat the dollar figures as a model to adjust, not as a result.

    Assumptions¶

    The Primary Author set two of these assumptions: the three benchmark stages are 10% of the cost of a whole book, and the service keeps a 20% profit margin. Claude, the AI assistant that wrote this page, chose the others.

    Purchase and Setup¶

    Assumption Value Basis
    Hardware price $6,950 NVIDIA's list price for a 128 GB DGX Spark in October 2026. It was $3,999 at launch in October 2025 and $4,699 from February 2026. Partner systems sold for $7,000 to $9,000
    Accessories $250 An uninterruptible power supply and network cabling. Assumed
    Service life 3 years, no resale value Assumed. The cost is spread evenly over the three years
    Setup labor 40 hours Assumed: 16 hours to connect, take the inventory, install the model server, and build the harness container, then 24 hours to test candidate models and choose one
    Labor rate $90 an hour Assumed, as a full cost with benefits for a technical employee

    Running¶

    Assumption Value Basis
    Share of hours spent generating 50% Assumed. The other half covers waiting for orders, failed and repeated runs, and maintenance
    Books in progress at once One per unit The evaluation ran one model and one session per unit
    Power while generating 150 W A published review measured 60 to 90 W in many model workloads and just under 200 W at most. 150 W is deliberately high. It was not measured at the wall on these units
    Power while idle 40 W The same review measured 40 to 45 W
    Electricity price $0.1385 per kWh The U.S. average commercial price for the 12 months ending July 2026
    Operations labor 4 hours a month Assumed: updates, new models, and restarting runs that fail
    Network and space $20 a month Assumed share of an existing office's internet and floor space

    Workload¶

    Assumption Value Basis
    Book size 200 concepts, 13 chapters, 55,000 words of chapter text The published Algebra I book that the benchmark uses as its reference for scale
    Tokens generated in the three stages 200,000 The chapter text is about 73,000 tokens at 1.33 tokens per word. The rest is an allowance for the learning graph, the chapter outlines, reasoning, tool calls, and rewrites
    Turns in the three stages 280 55 measured in stage 1, 30 assumed for stage 2, and 15 assumed for each of 13 chapters in stage 3
    Overhead per turn 15 seconds Assumed, for reading the new part of the prompt and running the tool. gemma4:26b read 3,000 prompt tokens a second
    Overhead per turn, qwen3.8:27b 90 seconds Measured: the server re-read the conversation on every turn, which added one to two minutes
    Share of the book in the three stages 10% The Primary Author's assumption. The learning graph, chapter design, and chapter content are one tenth of the cost. MicroSims, the glossary, the FAQ, quizzes, and references are the rest
    Profit margin 20% of the price The Primary Author's assumption

    Purchase, Setup, and Running Costs¶

    Buying and setting up one unit takes $10,800 before the first book: $7,200 for the hardware and accessories, and $3,600 for 40 hours of setup labor.

    Spread over three years and added to the running costs, one unit costs $8,275 a year.

    Cost Calculation Per year Share
    Hardware ($6,950 + $250) ÷ 3 years $2,400 29%
    Setup 40 hours × $90 ÷ 3 years $1,200 15%
    Electricity 832 kWh × $0.1385 $115 1%
    Operations labor 4 hours × 12 months × $90 $4,320 52%
    Network and space $20 × 12 months $240 3%
    Total $8,275 100%

    The electricity figure is 4,380 hours at 150 W plus 4,380 hours at 40 W, which is 832 kWh a year.

    At 50% of the 8,760 hours in a year, the unit generates for 4,380 hours. Each generating hour therefore costs $8,275 ÷ 4,380, or $1.89.

    People are the largest cost. Labor for setup and operations is two thirds of the total, and electricity is about one percent.

    Models, Sizes, and Speeds¶

    The table lists the ten model candidates from the smallest download to the largest. Four speeds were measured in this evaluation. The other six models have not been run, so each takes the speed of the tested model it most resembles.

    Model Parameters Download Generation speed Basis for the speed
    devstral-small-2:24b 24 billion 15 GB 34 tokens/s Assumed equal to gemma4:31b
    qwen3.8:27b 27 billion 17 GB 26 tokens/s Measured on the longest turn of its stage 1 run
    gemma4:26b 25.2 billion, 3.8 billion active 18 GB 108 tokens/s Measured: the midpoint of 86 to 130
    muse-glimmer:30b 30 billion 18 GB 34 tokens/s Assumed equal to gemma4:31b
    granite4.2:30b about 30 billion, dense 18 GB 34 tokens/s Assumed equal to gemma4:31b
    gemma4:31b 30.7 billion, dense 20 GB 34 tokens/s Measured: the midpoint of 28 to 40
    qwen3.6:35b 35 billion, about 3 billion active 24 GB 108 tokens/s Assumed equal to gemma4:26b
    nemotron-3.5-lightning:30b 30 billion, 3 billion active 25 GB 108 tokens/s Assumed equal to gemma4:26b
    gpt-oss:120b 117 billion, 5.1 billion active 65 GB 41 tokens/s Measured: the midpoint of 38 to 44
    mistral-medium-3.5:128b 128 billion, dense 80 GB 8.5 tokens/s Assumed: one quarter of gemma4:31b, because it has four times the weights to read for each token

    Three rules stand behind the assumed speeds.

    • A mixture-of-experts model of about 30 billion parameters, with 3 to 4 billion active, runs like gemma4:26b.
    • A model of 24 to 31 billion parameters with no published active count is treated as dense and runs like gemma4:31b.
    • A dense model's speed falls in proportion to the size of its weights.

    These rules are rough. The second one predicts 34 tokens a second for qwen3.8:27b, and the measured figure was 26.

    Download size has almost no effect on cost. Every model fits in 128 GB, and memory is paid for whether a model uses it or not. What a larger model costs is time.

    Time to Generate a Textbook¶

    The time for the three benchmark stages has two parts: the time to generate the tokens, and the overhead of each turn.

    three-stage time = 200,000 tokens ÷ generation speed
                 + 280 turns × overhead per turn

whole-book time  = three-stage time ÷ 10%

    The unit costs the same for every hour it generates, so a tenth of the cost is a tenth of the machine time. A whole book takes ten times as long as the three stages.

    The one measured stage checks the overhead figure. Stage 1 for qwen3.8:27b made 55 tool calls. At 90 seconds each that is 83 minutes, and one turn of reasoning took another 19 minutes. The sum is 102 minutes, which is the time the stage took.

    Many of the models share a speed, so the ten models reduce to six cases.

    Case Generating Turn overhead Three stages Whole book
    gemma4:26b, and by assumption qwen3.6:35b and nemotron-3.5-lightning:30b 31 minutes 70 minutes 1.7 hours 17 hours
    gpt-oss:120b 81 minutes 70 minutes 2.5 hours 25 hours
    gemma4:31b, and by assumption devstral-small-2:24b, muse-glimmer:30b, and granite4.2:30b 98 minutes 70 minutes 2.8 hours 28 hours
    qwen3.8:27b, if the re-reading is fixed 128 minutes 70 minutes 3.3 hours 33 hours
    mistral-medium-3.5:128b 392 minutes 70 minutes 7.7 hours 77 hours
    qwen3.8:27b, as measured 128 minutes 420 minutes 9.1 hours 91 hours

    Two things stand out. For the fastest models, turn overhead is longer than generation, so a faster model saves less than its speed suggests. And the slowest case is not the largest model. It is a 27-billion-parameter model on a server that re-reads the conversation on every turn.

    Cost per Textbook and the Price at a 20% Margin¶

    A book's cost is its machine time at $1.89 an hour. The price that leaves a 20% margin is the cost divided by 0.80, because the margin is a share of the price, not of the cost.

    cost per book  = whole-book hours × $1.89
price per book = cost per book ÷ (1 − 0.20)
    Case Whole book Books per year Cost per book Price at a 20% margin
    gemma4:26b, qwen3.6:35b, nemotron-3.5-lightning:30b 17 hours 260 $32 $40
    gpt-oss:120b 25 hours 174 $48 $60
    gemma4:31b, devstral-small-2:24b, muse-glimmer:30b, granite4.2:30b 28 hours 156 $53 $66
    qwen3.8:27b, if the re-reading is fixed 33 hours 133 $62 $78
    mistral-medium-3.5:128b 77 hours 57 $146 $182
    qwen3.8:27b, as measured 91 hours 48 $173 $216

    Books per year is 4,380 generating hours divided by the hours for one book.

    The reference case on this page is gemma4:31b. Its speed was measured, it is near the middle of the four measured speeds, and it is the only model whose skill runs all ended with a site that builds. It has not finished a benchmark stage.

    In the reference case one unit produces 156 books a year. At $66 each that is about $10,300 in revenue against $8,275 in cost, which leaves about $2,100 in profit. A 20% markup on cost is a different and lower number: it gives a price of $63 and a margin of 16.7%.

    Of the $53, electricity is 58 cents.

    What Moves the Answer¶

    Each row changes one assumption in the reference case and leaves the others alone.

    Change Cost per book Price at a 20% margin
    Reference case $53 $66
    Generating 75% of the time: 235 books a year $35 $44
    Generating 25% of the time: 78 books a year $105 $132
    Generating 10% of the time: 31 books a year $263 $329
    No operations labor $25 $32
    Operations labor of 8 hours a month $81 $101
    Hardware at the launch price of $3,999 $47 $58
    Hardware at $9,000 $57 $72
    A five-year life for the hardware and setup $44 $55
    The three stages are 20% of the book $26 $33
    The three stages are 5% of the book $106 $132

    Nearly all of the cost is fixed, so the cost of a book is close to the yearly cost divided by the number of books sold. A unit that sells 31 books a year has to charge five times what a unit that sells 156 does. Demand, labor, and the 10% share move the price far more than the hardware price does, and the electricity price hardly moves it at all.

    Throughput is the other lever. This analysis has each unit generate one book at a time. A server that batches several sessions together could produce more books from the same hardware. vLLM is the candidate named in Design Choices, and it has not been tested.

    What the Price Leaves Out¶

    • Human review. The price covers generation only. Ten hours of review by a subject expert at the same $90 an hour would add $900, seventeen times the cost of generating the book.
    • Stages that have not been run. The 10% share is an assumption about work no local model has attempted here. If a model cannot build working MicroSims, the other 90% of the book has no price at all.
    • The cost of selling. Marketing, customer support, payment fees, and taxes are not included.
    • Financing and sales tax on the hardware.
    • License terms. A service sells what the model writes. Several candidates state no license or attach conditions to commercial use, and the harness's terms for this use were not reviewed.
    • Supervision by a cloud model. In this evaluation a cloud assistant started and watched the runs. A service would replace it with scripts, and its cost is not counted.

    This page does not compare the result with generating the same book in the cloud. The published Algebra I book was made with cloud models, and no token counts or timings were kept for it.
