    1. Algebraic Expressions¶

    An algebraic expression is a combination of numbers, variables, and operations (such as addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and exponentiation) that represents a mathematical value. Algebraic expressions are the fundamental building blocks of algebra; they allow us to represent unknown quantities and relationships between quantities symbolically.

    Before we work with diagrams or examples, let us define some key terms that will appear throughout this chapter:

    • Variable — A symbol, usually a letter such as \(x\), \(y\), or \(n\), that represents an unknown number or a number that can change.
    • Constant — A fixed value that does not change. In the expression \(3x + 5\), the number \(5\) is a constant.
    • Coefficient — A number that multiplies a variable. In the term \(3x\), the number \(3\) is the coefficient of \(x\).
    • Term — A single number, a variable, or numbers and variables multiplied together. In the expression \(3x + 5\), there are two terms: \(3x\) and \(5\).
    • Algebraic expression — A combination of one or more terms connected by addition or subtraction.

    Worked Example 1: Identifying Parts of an Expression¶

    Identify the coefficient, variable, constant, and number of terms in the expression \(4x - 7\).

    Solution: The expression \(4x - 7\) has two terms: \(4x\) and \(-7\). The coefficient of \(x\) is \(4\). The constant term is \(-7\). The variable is \(x\).

    Answer: Coefficient: \(4\); Variable: \(x\); Constant: \(-7\); Number of terms: \(2\).

    Diagram: Expression Parts¶

    Expression Parts Diagram

    An algebraic expression \(4x - 7\) is visualized as a two-term expression connected by subtraction. The first term \(4x\) consists of a coefficient \(4\) multiplied by a variable \(x\). The second term \(-7\) is a constant value.

    Expanding Algebraic Expressions¶

    To expand an algebraic expression means to use the distributive property to rewrite a product of a sum as a sum of products. For example, to expand \(2(x + 3)\), we multiply \(2\) by each term inside the parentheses: \(2(x + 3) = 2x + 6\).

    Factor Algebraic Expressions¶

    To factor an algebraic expression means to rewrite it as a product of simpler expressions. For example, the expression \(x^2 + 5x + 6\) can be factored as \((x + 2)(x + 3)\), because \((x + 2)(x + 3) = x^2 + 5x + 6\).

    Evaluate Expressions With a Variable¶

    To evaluate an algebraic expression means to substitute a given number for the variable and then simplify the resulting expression using the order of operations.

    Integer Exponents¶

    An integer exponent tells us how many times to use the base as a factor. For example, \(2^3\) means \(2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 8\). The base is \(2\) and the exponent is \(3\). Integer exponents follow these rules:

    • \(a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n}\)
    • \((a^m)^n = a^{mn}\)
    • \(a^m / a^n = a^{m-n}\) (for \(a \neq 0\))

    Law of Exponents¶

    The laws of exponents are a set of rules that help us simplify expressions involving exponents. The five main laws are:

    1. Product of Powers: \(a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n}\)
    2. Quotient of Powers: \(a^m / a^n = a^{m-n}\) (for \(a \neq 0\))
    3. Power of a Power: \((a^m)^n = a^{mn}\)
    4. Power of a Product: \((ab)^n = a^n b^n\)
    5. Power of a Quotient: \((a/b)^n = a^n / b^n\) (for \(b \neq 0\))

    Rational Exponents¶

    A rational exponent is an exponent that is a fraction. The expression \(a^{m/n}\) can be rewritten using radicals: \(a^{m/n} = \sqrt[n]{a^m} = (\sqrt[n]{a})^m\). For example, \(8^{2/3} = \sqrt[3]{8^2} = \sqrt[3]{64} = 4\).

    Simplify Exponential Expressions¶

    To simplify an exponential expression means to use the laws of exponents to rewrite the expression in a simpler or more compact form. For example, to simplify \(2^3 \cdot 2^4\), we use the product of powers rule: \(2^3 \cdot 2^4 = 2^{3+4} = 2^7 = 128\).

    Solve One-Step Equations¶

    A one-step equation is an equation that can be solved by performing a single operation to isolate the variable. For example, to solve \(x + 5 = 12\), we subtract \(5\) from both sides: \(x = 12 - 5 = 7\). The solution is \(x = 7\).

    Solve Two-Step Equations¶

    A two-step equation is an equation that requires two operations to isolate the variable. For example, to solve \(2x + 3 = 11\), we first subtract \(3\) from both sides: \(2x = 8\), and then divide both sides by \(2\): \(x = 4\). The solution is \(x = 4\).

    Solve Multi-Step Equations¶

    A multi-step equation is an equation that requires more than two operations to isolate the variable. The key strategy is to simplify each side of the equation as much as possible, then use inverse operations to isolate the variable, working in reverse of the order of operations. For example, to solve \(3(x - 2) + 4 = 13\):

    1. Subtract \(4\) from both sides: \(3(x - 2) = 9\)
    2. Divide both sides by \(3\): \(x - 2 = 3\)
    3. Add \(2\) to both sides: \(x = 5\)

    The solution is \(x = 5\).

    Literal Equations¶

    A literal equation is an equation that involves two or more variables. Solving a literal equation means isolating one variable in terms of the others. For example, to solve the literal equation \(A = lw\) for \(w\), we divide both sides by \(l\): \(w = A / l\).

    Formula Rearrangement¶

    Formula rearrangement is the process of rearranging a formula to solve for a different variable than the one originally highlighted. This skill is essential for applying mathematical models to real-world problems. For example, the formula for the area of a rectangle is \(A = lw\). If we want to solve for the width \(w\), we rearrange the formula by dividing both sides by the length \(l\): \(w = A / l\). Similarly, if we want to solve for the length \(l\), we rearrange by dividing both sides by the width \(w\): \(l = A / w\).

    2. Order of Operations¶

    The order of operations is a set of rules that determines the order in which operations are performed in a mathematical expression. Without a standard order, different people might simplify the same expression to different results. The standard order is:

    1. Parentheses: Simplify expressions inside parentheses or other grouping symbols first.
    2. Exponents: Evaluate all exponents (powers and roots).
    3. Multiplication and Division: Perform multiplication and division from left to right.
    4. Addition and Subtraction: Perform addition and subtraction from left to right.

    A helpful mnemonic for remembering the order is PEMDAS: Parentheses, Exponents, Multiplication/Division, Addition/Subtraction.

    Worked Example 2: Using the Order of Operations¶

    Simplify the expression \(3 + 4 \cdot 2^2\) using the order of operations.

    Solution:

    1. Parentheses: There are no parentheses to simplify, so we move to the next step.
    2. Exponents: Evaluate \(2^2 = 4\). The expression becomes \(3 + 4 \cdot 4\).
    3. Multiplication and Division: Multiply \(4 \cdot 4 = 16\). The expression becomes \(3 + 16\).
    4. Addition and Subtraction: Add \(3 + 16 = 19\).

    Answer: \(19\)

    Worked Example 3: Another Order of Operations Problem¶

    Simplify \(2 + 3 \cdot (4 + 1)\).

    Solution:

    1. Parentheses: Simplify inside the parentheses first: \(4 + 1 = 5\).
    2. Exponents: There are no exponents to evaluate.
    3. Multiplication and Division: Multiply \(3 \cdot 5 = 15\).
    4. Addition and Subtraction: Add \(2 + 15 = 17\).

    Answer: \(17\)

    Integer Exponents (continued)¶

    Integer exponents were introduced in the previous section, but it is worth reinforcing key points here. When the exponent is \(0\), the result is \(1\) (for any nonzero base): \(a^0 = 1\) (for \(a \neq 0\). When the exponent is negative, the result is the reciprocal: \(a^{-n} = 1 / a^n\). For example, \(2^{-3} = 1 / 2^3 = 1 / 8 = 0.125\).

    Law of Exponents (continued)¶

    The laws of exponents can be applied in many contexts. For example, to simplify the expression \((2^3 \cdot 2^4) / 2^2\), we first use the product of powers rule: \(2^3 \cdot 2^4 = 2^{3+4} = 2^7\). Then we use the quotient of powers rule: \(2^7 / 2^2 = 2^{7-2} = 2^5 = 32\).

    Rational Exponents (continued)¶

    Rational exponents provide a convenient way to express roots using exponent notation. The denominator of the fraction indicates the index of the root, and the numerator indicates the power. For example, \(16^{1/2} = \sqrt{16} = 4\), and \(27^{2/3} = (\sqrt[3]{27})^2 = 3^2 = 9\).

    Simplify Exponential Expressions (continued)¶

    When simplifying expressions with rational exponents, we can convert between radical and exponential form as needed. For example, to simplify \(\sqrt{x^3}\), we write it as \(x^{3/2}\). To simplify \((x^{1/2})^3\), we multiply the exponents: \((x^{1/2})^3 = x^{(1/2) \cdot 3} = x^{3/2}\).

    Solve One-Step Equations (continued)¶

    When solving one-step equations, the goal is to isolate the variable on one side of the equation using the inverse operation. If the equation involves addition, we subtract; if it involves subtraction, we add; if it involves multiplication, we divide; if it involves division, we multiply. For example:

    • \(x - 3 = 10\): Add \(3\) to both sides to get \(x = 13\).
    • \(5x = 20\): Divide both sides by \(5\) to get \(x = 4\).
    • \(x / 4 = 3\): Multiply both sides by \(4\) to get \(x = 12\).

    Solve Two-Step Equations (continued)¶

    The key to solving two-step equations is to reverse the order of operations: first undo addition or subtraction, then undo multiplication or division. For example, to solve \(2x + 7 = 15\):

    1. Subtract \(7\) from both sides: \(2x = 8\)
    2. Divide both sides by \(2\): \(x = 4\)

    Solve Multi-Step Equations (continued)¶

    When solving multi-step equations, it is often helpful to simplify each side of the equation before isolating the variable. For example, to solve \(2(x + 3) - 4 = 10\):

    1. Distribute the \(2\): \(2x + 6 - 4 = 10\)
    2. Combine like terms: \(2x + 2 = 10\)
    3. Subtract \(2\) from both sides: \(2x = 8\)
    4. Divide both sides by \(2\): \(x = 4\)

    Literal Equations (continued)¶

    Rearranging literal equations follows the same principles as solving regular equations, but the goal is to isolate a specific variable. For example, to solve the formula for the perimeter of a rectangle \(P = 2l + 2w\) for \(l\):

    1. Subtract \(2w\) from both sides: \(P - 2w = 2l\)
    2. Divide both sides by \(2\): \(l = (P - 2w) / 2\)

    Formula Rearrangement (continued)¶

    Formula rearrangement is used extensively in science and engineering. For example, the formula for the volume of a cylinder is \(V = \pi r^2 h\). If we want to solve for the height \(h\), we rearrange: \(h = V / (\pi r^2)\). If we want to solve for the radius \(r\), we rearrange: \(r = \sqrt{V / (\pi h)}\).

    3. Variables and Variable Identification¶

    A variable is a symbol, usually a letter, that represents a number that can change or that we don't yet know. Variables are used to represent unknown quantities in algebraic expressions, equations, and inequalities. The most common variables are \(x\), \(y\), \(z\), \(a\), \(b\), \(m\), and \(n\), but any letter can serve as a variable.

    Worked Example 4: Identifying Variables¶

    Identify the variables in the expression \(3x + 5y - 2\).

    Solution: The variables in the expression \(3x + 5y - 2\) are \(x\) and \(y\). The number \(3\) is a coefficient, the number \(5\) is a coefficient, and the number \(-2\) is a constant term.

    Answer: Variables: \(x, y\)

    Coefficient Recognition¶

    A coefficient is a number that multiplies a variable in a term. In the term \(7x\), the coefficient is \(7\). If a variable appears without a visible coefficient, the coefficient is \(1\). For example, in the term \(x\), the coefficient is \(1\), because \(x = 1 \cdot x\). If a variable appears with a negative sign, the coefficient is \(-1\). For example, in the term \(-y\), the coefficient is \(-1\).

    Constant Term¶

    A constant term is a term in an algebraic expression that contains no variables; its value is fixed. In the expression \(3x + 5\), the constant term is \(5\). Constant terms are important because they represent y-intercepts in graphs of linear functions, and they help us understand the starting value in many real-world situations.

    Expand Algebraic Expression (continued)¶

    When we expand an expression, we use the distributive property: \(a(b + c) = ab + ac\). For example, to expand \(3(x + 4)\), we multiply \(3\) by both \(x\) and \(4\): \(3(x + 4) = 3x + 12\).

    Factor Algebraic Expression (continued)¶

    Factoring is the reverse of expanding. To factor the quadratic expression \(x^2 + 5x + 6\), we look for two numbers that multiply to \(6\) and add to \(5\). Those numbers are \(2\) and \(3\), so \(x^2 + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3)\).

    Evaluate Expression With Variable (continued)¶

    When evaluating expressions with variables, we substitute the given value for the variable and then simplify using the order of operations. For example, to evaluate \(3x + 2\) when \(x = 5\):

    1. Substitute \(5\) for \(x\): \(3(5) + 2\)
    2. Multiply: \(15 + 2\)
    3. Add: \(17\)

    Answer: \(17\)

    4. Constant Term (Detailed)¶

    The constant term is a crucial concept in algebra. It is a number on its own, or sometimes a letter such as \(a\), \(b\), or \(c\) that stands for a fixed number. For example, in the expression \(3x + 7\), the constant term is \(7\). In the expression \(ax + b\), the constant term is \(b\).

    Constant terms play several important roles:

    • In a linear equation \(y = mx + b\), the constant \(b\) is the \(y\)-intercept, which is where the graph crosses the \(y\)-axis.
    • In a quadratic function \(f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c\), the constant \(c\) is the value of the function when \(x = 0\).
    • Constant terms help us understand the starting value in real-world situations, such as an initial amount of money, an initial height, or a base value.

    Worked Example 5: Identifying the Constant Term¶

    Identify the constant term in the expression \(4x^2 - 3x + 2\).

    Solution: The expression \(4x^2 - 3x + 2\) has three terms: \(4x^2\), \(-3x\), and \(2\). The term \(2\) does not contain the variable \(x\), so it is the constant term.

    Answer: Constant term: \(2\)

    Expand Algebraic Expression (continued)¶

    Expanding expressions with more than two terms follows the same distributive property. For example, to expand \(2(x + 3y + 4)\), we multiply \(2\) by each term inside the parentheses: \(2(x + 3y + 4) = 2x + 6y + 8\).

    Factor Algebraic Expression (continued)¶

    Factoring quadratic expressions of the form \(x^2 + bx + c\) involves finding two numbers that multiply to \(c\) and add to \(b\). For example, to factor \(x^2 + 7x + 10\), we look for two numbers that multiply to \(10\) and add to \(7\). Those numbers are \(2\) and \(5\), so \(x^2 + 7x + 10 = (x + 2)(x + 5)\).

    Evaluate Expression With Variable (continued)¶

    When evaluating expressions with multiple variables, substitute the given values for each variable and then simplify. For example, to evaluate \(2x + 3y\) when \(x = 4\) and \(y = 5\):

    1. Substitute \(4\) for \(x\) and \(5\) for \(y\): \(2(4) + 3(5)\)
    2. Multiply: \(8 + 15\)
    3. Add: \(23\)

    Answer: \(23\)

    Integer Exponents (continued)¶

    Additional exponent rules include the zero exponent rule and the negative exponent rule:

    • Zero exponent: \(a^0 = 1\) for any \(a \neq 0\)
    • Negative exponent: \(a^{-n} = 1 / a^n\)

    For example, \(5^0 = 1\) and \(2^{-3} = 1 / 2^3 = 1 / 8 = 0.125\).

    Law of Exponents (continued)¶

    Additional applications of the laws of exponents include simplifying expressions with multiple variables. For example, to simplify \((xy)^3\), we use the power of a product rule: \((xy)^3 = x^3 y^3\).

    Rational Exponents (continued)¶

    When working with rational exponents, it is often helpful to convert between radical form and exponent form. For example:

    • \(\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}\)
    • \(\sqrt[3]{x} = x^{1/3}\)
    • \(\sqrt[4]{x^3} = x^{3/4}\)

    Simplify Exponential Expressions (continued)¶

    To simplify expressions with rational exponents, we can use the rules of exponents. For example, to simplify \(x^{1/2} \cdot x^{1/3}\), we add the exponents: \(x^{1/2} \cdot x^{1/3} = x^{1/2 + 1/3} = x^{5/6}\).

    Solve One-Step Equation (continued)¶

    When solving one-step equations with multiplication or division, we use the inverse operation. For example:

    • \(x / 5 = 3\): Multiply both sides by \(5\) to get \(x = 15\).
    • \(4x = 28\): Divide both sides by \(4\) to get \(x = 7\).

    Solve Two-Step Equation (continued)¶

    When solving two-step equations, always undo addition/subtraction before multiplication/division. For example, to solve \(x / 3 + 2 = 5\):

    1. Subtract \(2\) from both sides: \(x / 3 = 3\)
    2. Multiply both sides by \(3\): \(x = 9\)

    Solve Multi-Step Equation (continued)¶

    A key strategy for multi-step equations is to work step by step, simplifying each time. For example, to solve \(4(x - 1) + 2 = 14\):

    1. Subtract \(2\) from both sides: \(4(x - 1) = 12\)
    2. Divide both sides by \(4\): \(x - 1 = 3\)
    3. Add \(1\) to both sides: \(x = 4\)

    Literal Equation (continued)¶

    When solving literal equations, treat the variable you are isolating as the only variable and all others as constants. For example, to solve \(A = \pi r^2\) for \(r\):

    1. Divide both sides by \(\pi\): \(A / \pi = r^2\)
    2. Take the square root of both sides: \(r = \sqrt{A / \pi}\)

    Formula Rearrangement (continued)¶

    Rearranging formulas is a skill that becomes easier with practice. The key steps are:

    1. Identify the variable you want to solve for.
    2. Use inverse operations to isolate that variable.
    3. Simplify the resulting expression.

    For example, the formula for the surface area of a rectangular prism is \(SA = 2lw + 2lh + 2wh\). To solve for \(h\):

    1. Subtract \(2lw\) from both sides: \(SA - 2lw = 2lh + 2wh\)
    2. Factor out \(h\): \(SA - 2lw = h(2l + 2w)\)
    3. Divide both sides by \((2l + 2w)\): \(h = (SA - 2lw) / (2l + 2w)\)

    5. Equations and Inequalities¶

    An equation is a mathematical statement that asserts that two expressions are equal. Equations are used to model relationships between quantities and to find unknown values. A solution to an equation is a value of the variable that makes the equation true.

    An inequality is a mathematical statement that compares two expressions using inequality symbols: \(<\) (less than), \(>\) (greater than), \(\leq\) (less than or equal to), and \(\geq\) (greater than or equal to). Inequalities are used to represent constraints and conditions in real-world problems.

    Worked Example 6: Solving a Simple Equation¶

    Solve the equation \(x + 7 = 12\).

    Solution: To isolate \(x\), subtract \(7\) from both sides of the equation:

    \[x + 7 - 7 = 12 - 7$$ $$x = 5\]

    Answer: \(x = 5\)

    Absolute Value Equation¶

    An absolute value equation is an equation that contains an absolute value expression. The absolute value of a number is its distance from zero on the number line, regardless of direction. For example, \(|3| = 3\) and \(|-3| = 3\). To solve an absolute value equation, we consider both the positive and negative cases. For example, to solve \(|x| = 5\):

    • Case 1: \(x = 5\)
    • Case 2: \(x = -5\)

    The solutions are \(x = 5\) and \(x = -5\).

    Absolute Value Inequality¶

    An absolute value inequality is an inequality that contains an absolute value expression. For example, to solve \(|x| < 3\):

    • The distance of \(x\) from \(0\) is less than \(3\), so \(x\) is between \(-3\) and \(3\).
    • In interval notation, the solution is \((-3, 3)\).

    Function Concept¶

    A function is a relation between a set of inputs and a set of permissible outputs with the property that each input is related to exactly one output. Functions are often represented using function notation \(f(x)\), where \(f\) is the name of the function and \(x\) is the input variable.

    Function Notation¶

    Function notation is a way to represent functions using the symbol \(f(x)\). The expression \(f(x)\) reads as "f of x" and represents the output of the function \(f\) when the input is \(x\). For example, if \(f(x) = 2x + 3\), then \(f(4) = 2(4) + 3 = 11\).

    Domain of Function¶

    The domain of a function is the set of all possible input values (values of the independent variable) for which the function is defined. For example, the domain of \(f(x) = 1/x\) is all real numbers except \(x = 0\), because division by zero is undefined.

    Range of Function¶

    The range of a function is the set of all possible output values (values of the dependent variable) that the function can produce. For example, the range of \(f(x) = x^2\) is all nonnegative real numbers, because squaring any real number always gives a nonnegative result.

    Interpret Function Graph¶

    To interpret a function graph, we look at the visual representation of the function to understand its behavior, such as where it is increasing or decreasing, where it has maximum or minimum values, and what its domain and range are.

    Interpret Function Table¶

    A function table (or input-output table) lists input values and their corresponding output values for a function. For example, if \(f(x) = 2x + 1\), a function table might look like this:

    \(x\) \(f(x)\)
    \(-2\) \(-3\)
    \(-1\) \(-1\)
    \(0\) \(1\)
    \(1\) \(3\)
    \(2\) \(5\)

    Piecewise Function¶

    A piecewise function is a function defined by different expressions for different intervals of the domain. For example:

    \[f(x) = \begin{cases} x + 2 & \text{if } x < 0 \\ x^2 & \text{if } x \geq 0 \end{cases}\]

    This function uses the expression \(x + 2\) when \(x\) is less than \(0\), and the expression \(x^2\) when \(x\) is greater than or equal to \(0\).

    Step Function¶

    A step function is a piecewise function that has a constant value over each interval of its domain, so the graph looks like a series of steps. The greatest integer function, denoted \(f(x) = \lfloor x \rfloor\), is a common step function that gives the greatest integer less than or equal to \(x\).

    Slope and Rate of Change¶

    The slope of a line is a number that measures its steepness. The slope is defined as the ratio of the vertical change (rise) to the horizontal change (run) between two points on the line. The formula for slope is:

    \[m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}\]

    The rate of change describes how one quantity changes in relation to another quantity. In a linear function, the rate of change is constant and equal to the slope.

    Slope-Intercept Form¶

    The slope-intercept form of a linear equation is \(y = mx + b\), where \(m\) is the slope and \(b\) is the \(y\)-intercept. This form is useful for graphing linear equations and understanding the relationship between the slope and the \(y\)-intercept.

    Point-Slope Form¶

    The point-slope form of a linear equation is \(y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)\), where \(m\) is the slope and \((x_1, y_1)\) is a point on the line. This form is useful for writing the equation of a line when you know the slope and one point on the line.

    Standard Form Equation¶

    The standard form of a linear equation is \(Ax + By = C\), where \(A\), \(B\), and \(C\) are integers, and \(A\) is nonnegative. This form is useful for certain algebraic manipulations and for finding intercepts.

    Graph Linear Equation¶

    To graph a linear equation, we can find two points that satisfy the equation and draw a line through them. Alternatively, we can use the slope and \(y\)-intercept to plot the line starting from the \(y\)-intercept and using the slope to find another point.

    Model Linear Relationship¶

    To model a linear relationship is to use a linear equation to represent a real-world situation where one quantity changes at a constant rate relative to another quantity. For example, if a car travels at a constant speed of \(60\) miles per hour, the distance \(d\) traveled after \(t\) hours can be modeled by the equation \(d = 60t\).

    System of Linear Equations¶

    A system of linear equations consists of two or more linear equations involving the same set of variables. The solution to a system of linear equations is the set of values that satisfy all equations in the system simultaneously. There are three possible outcomes:

    1. One solution: The lines intersect at exactly one point.
    2. No solution: The lines are parallel and never intersect.
    3. Infinite solutions: The lines are the same line.

    System of Linear Inequalities¶

    A system of linear inequalities consists of two or more linear inequalities involving the same set of variables. The solution to a system of linear inequalities is the set of all points that satisfy all inequalities in the system simultaneously. The graph of a system of linear inequalities is the intersection of the half-planes defined by each inequality.

    6. Polynomials and Factoring¶

    A polynomial is an algebraic expression consisting of one or more terms, where each term is a constant multiplied by a variable raised to a nonnegative integer exponent. For example, \(3x^2 + 5x - 7\) is a polynomial with three terms.

    Polynomial Addition¶

    To add polynomials, we combine like terms. Like terms are terms that have the same variable raised to the same power. For example, to add \((3x^2 + 2x + 1) + (x^2 - x + 4)\):

    1. Group like terms: \((3x^2 + x^2) + (2x - x) + (1 + 4)\)
    2. Simplify: \(4x^2 + x + 5\)

    Polynomial Subtraction¶

    To subtract polynomials, we distribute the negative sign and then combine like terms. For example, to subtract \((x^2 - 2x + 3)\) from \((3x^2 + 4x - 5)\):

    1. Distribute the negative sign: \((3x^2 + 4x - 5) - x^2 + 2x - 3\)
    2. Group like terms: \((3x^2 - x^2) + (4x + 2x) + (-5 - 3)\)
    3. Simplify: \(2x^2 + 6x - 8\)

    Polynomial Multiplication¶

    To multiply polynomials, we use the distributive property (also known as the FOIL method for binomials). For example, to multiply \((x + 2)(x + 3)\):

    1. First: \(x \cdot x = x^2\)
    2. Outer: \(x \cdot 3 = 3x\)
    3. Inner: \(2 \cdot x = 2x\)
    4. Last: \(2 \cdot 3 = 6\)
    5. Combine like terms: \(x^2 + 5x + 6\)

    Greatest Common Factor¶

    The greatest common factor (GCF) of a polynomial is the largest polynomial that divides evenly into each term. For example, the GCF of \(6x^2 + 9x\) is \(3x\), because \(6x^2 / 3x = 2x\) and \(9x / 3x = 3\). Factoring out the GCF gives \(6x^2 + 9x = 3x(2x + 3)\).

    Factor Trinomial¶

    To factor a trinomial of the form \(x^2 + bx + c\), we look for two numbers that multiply to \(c\) and add to \(b\). For example, to factor \(x^2 + 5x + 6\), we look for two numbers that multiply to \(6\) and add to \(5\). Those numbers are \(2\) and \(3\), so \(x^2 + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3)\).

    Difference of Squares¶

    The difference of squares is a special factoring formula: \(a^2 - b^2 = (a + b)(a - b)\). For example, to factor \(x^2 - 9\), we recognize that \(x^2\) is a square and \(9\) is a square: \(x^2 - 9 = (x + 3)(x - 3)\).

    Solve Quadratic By Factoring¶

    To solve a quadratic equation by factoring, we first factor the quadratic expression and then use the zero-product property: if \(ab = 0\), then \(a = 0\) or \(b = 0\). For example, to solve \(x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0\):

    1. Factor the quadratic: \(x^2 - 5x + 6 = (x - 2)(x - 3)\)
    2. Set each factor equal to zero: \(x - 2 = 0\) or \(x - 3 = 0\)
    3. Solve each equation: \(x = 2\) or \(x = 3\)

    The solutions are \(x = 2\) and \(x = 3\).

    Solve Quadratic Square Root¶

    To solve a quadratic equation by taking the square root, we first isolate the squared term and then take the square root of both sides. For example, to solve \(x^2 = 16\):

    1. Take the square root of both sides: \(\sqrt{x^2} = \pm \sqrt{16}\)
    2. Simplify: \(x = \pm 4\)

    The solutions are \(x = 4\) and \(x = -4\).

    Complete Square Quadratic¶

    To complete the square for a quadratic expression \(x^2 + bx\), we add \((b/2)^2\) to create a perfect square trinomial. For example, to complete the square for \(x^2 + 6x\):

    1. Take half of the coefficient of \(x\): \(6 / 2 = 3\)
    2. Square it: \(3^2 = 9\)
    3. Add and subtract it: \(x^2 + 6x + 9 - 9 = (x + 3)^2 - 9\)

    Quadratic Formula¶

    The quadratic formula is a formula that gives the solutions of any quadratic equation \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\):

    \[x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2ac}\]

    The expression under the square root, \(b^2 - 4ac\), is called the discriminant. It tells us the nature of the solutions:

    • If \(b^2 - 4ac > 0\), there are two real solutions.
    • If \(b^2 - 4ac = 0\), there is one real solution.
    • If \(b^2 - 4ac < 0\), there are two complex solutions.

    Quadratic Vertex¶

    The vertex of a parabola given by \(y = ax^2 + bx + c\) is the point \((h, k)\), where \(h = -b / (2a)\) and \(k = f(h)\). The vertex is the highest or lowest point on the parabola, depending on the sign of \(a\).

    Quadratic Axis of Symmetry¶

    The axis of symmetry of a parabola is the vertical line that passes through the vertex. Its equation is \(x = -b / (2a)\).

    Model Quadratic Data¶

    To model quadratic data is to use a quadratic function to represent a set of data points that form a parabolic pattern. This is useful in many real-world situations, such as modeling the path of a projectile, the profit of a business, or the height of an object over time.

    7. Quadratic and Exponential Functions¶

    A quadratic function is a polynomial function of degree \(2\), typically written in the form \(f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c\), where \(a \neq 0\). The graph of a quadratic function is a parabola, which is a U-shaped curve that opens either upward or downward.

    A exponential function is a function of the form \(f(x) = a \cdot b^x\), where \(a \neq 0\) and \(b > 0\), \(b \neq 1\). In an exponential function, the variable \(x\) is in the exponent, which leads to rapid growth or decay.

    Graphing Quadratic Functions¶

    To graph a quadratic function, we can find the vertex, axis of symmetry, and a few points on either side of the vertex. The direction the parabola opens (upward or downward) is determined by the sign of \(a\): if \(a > 0\), the parabola opens upward; if \(a < 0\), the parabola opens downward.

    Identifying Vertex and Axis of Symmetry¶

    The vertex of a quadratic function \(f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c\) is at the point \(\left(-\frac{b}{2a}, f\left(-\frac{b}{2a}\right)\right)\). The axis of symmetry is the vertical line \(x = -\frac{b}{2a}\).

    Solving Quadratics by Factoring¶

    To solve a quadratic equation by factoring, we factor the quadratic expression and use the zero-product property. For example, to solve \(x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0\):

    1. Factor: \((x - 2)(x - 3) = 0\)
    2. Set each factor to zero: \(x - 2 = 0\) or \(x - 3 = 0\)
    3. Solve: \(x = 2\) or \(x = 3\)

    Solving Quadratics by Square Roots¶

    To solve a quadratic equation by taking square roots, we first isolate the \(x^2\) term and then take the square root of both sides. For example, to solve \(x^2 = 25\):

    1. Take the square root of both sides: \(x = \pm \sqrt{25}\)
    2. Simplify: \(x = \pm 5\)

    The solutions are \(x = 5\) and \(x = -5\).

    Solving Quadratics by Completing the Square¶

    To solve a quadratic equation by completing the square, we rewrite the equation in the form \((x - h)^2 = k\) and then take the square root of both sides. For example, to solve \(x^2 + 6x + 5 = 0\):

    1. Move the constant term: \(x^2 + 6x = -5\)
    2. Complete the square: \(x^2 + 6x + 9 = -5 + 9\)
    3. Factor: \((x + 3)^2 = 4\)
    4. Take the square root: \(x + 3 = \pm 2\)
    5. Solve: \(x = -3 \pm 2\), so \(x = -1\) or \(x = -5\)

    Solving Quadratics Using the Quadratic Formula¶

    To solve any quadratic equation \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\) using the quadratic formula, substitute the coefficients \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\) into the formula:

    \[x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}\]

    For example, to solve \(2x^2 + 3x - 2 = 0\):

    1. Identify \(a = 2\), \(b = 3\), \(c = -2\)
    2. Substitute into the formula: \(x = \frac{-3 \pm \sqrt{3^2 - 4(2)(-2)}}{2(2)}\)
    3. Simplify: \(x = \frac{-3 \pm \sqrt{9 + 16}}{4} = \frac{-3 \pm \sqrt{25}}{4}\)
    4. Simplify further: \(x = \frac{-3 \pm 5}{4}\)
    5. Solve: \(x = \frac{-3 + 5}{4} = \frac{2}{4} = \frac{1}{2}\) or \(x = \frac{-3 - 5}{4} = \frac{-8}{4} = -2\)

    The solutions are \(x = \frac{1}{2}\) and \(x = -2\).

    Modeling Real-World Data Using Quadratic and Exponential Functions¶

    Quadratic and exponential functions are used to model many real-world situations. Quadratic functions can model the path of a projectile, the area of a shape, or the profit of a business. Exponential functions can model population growth, radioactive decay, or compound interest.

    For example, the height \(h\) of a projectile \(t\) seconds after it is launched can be modeled by the quadratic function \(h(t) = -16t^2 + vt + s\), where \(v\) is the initial velocity and \(s\) is the initial height. The exponential function \(f(x) = 2^x\) models doubling behavior, such as bacterial growth or compound interest.

    8. Statistics and Data Analysis¶

    Statistics is the study of collecting, organizing, analyzing, and interpreting data. In this section, we will explore how to display data, measure relationships between variables, and compare different models.

    Creating and Interpreting Data Displays¶

    Data displays help us visualize and understand data. Common displays include:

    • Dot plots: A simple display where each data point is represented by a dot above a number line.
    • Histograms: A bar graph that shows the frequency of data within intervals.
    • Box plots: A display that shows the five-number summary (minimum, first quartile, median, third quartile, maximum) of a data set.
    • Scatterplots: A graph that plots pairs of data points on a coordinate plane to show relationships between two variables.

    Analyzing Correlation and Line of Best Fit¶

    Correlation measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship between two variables. The correlation coefficient \(r\) is a number between \(-1\) and \(1\) that measures the strength of the linear relationship:

    • \(r = 1\): Perfect positive linear relationship
    • \(r = -1\): Perfect negative linear relationship
    • \(r = 0\): No linear relationship

    The line of best fit (or regression line) is a line that approximates the trend in a scatterplot. It can be used to make predictions about one variable based on the value of another variable.

    Comparing Linear and Exponential Models¶

    When we have data that shows a constant rate of change, a linear model \(y = mx + b\) is appropriate. When the data shows a constant percent change, an exponential model \(y = a \cdot b^x\) is appropriate. To decide which model is better, we can look at the context of the data and the value of the correlation coefficient \(r\).

    Arithmetic Sequences¶

    An arithmetic sequence is a sequence of numbers in which the difference between consecutive terms is constant. This constant difference is called the common difference, denoted \(d\). For example, the sequence \(3, 7, 11, 15, 19, \ldots\) is an arithmetic sequence with common difference \(d = 4\).

    The \(n\)th term of an arithmetic sequence can be found using the explicit formula:

    \[a_n = a_1 + (n - 1)d\]

    where \(a_1\) is the first term and \(n\) is the term position.

    Geometric Sequences¶

    A geometric sequence is a sequence of numbers in which the ratio between consecutive terms is constant. This constant ratio is called the common ratio, denoted \(r\). For example, the sequence \(2, 6, 18, 54, 162, \ldots\) is a geometric sequence with common ratio \(r = 3\).

    The \(n\)th term of a geometric sequence can be found using the explicit formula:

    \[a_n = a_1 \cdot r^{n-1}\]

    where \(a_1\) is the first term and \(n\) is the term position.

    Sequence Term¶

    The nth term of a sequence is the value at position \(n\) in the sequence. For arithmetic sequences, the nth term formula is \(a_n = a_1 + (n - 1)d\). For geometric sequences, the nth term formula is \(a_n = a_1 \cdot r^{n-1}\).

    Sequence Recursive Rule¶

    A recursive rule defines each term of a sequence using the previous term(s). For an arithmetic sequence, the recursive rule is \(a_1 = \text{first term}\), \(a_n = a_{n-1} + d\) for \(n > 1\). For a geometric sequence, the recursive rule is \(a_1 = \text{first term}\), \(a_n = a_{n-1} \cdot r\) for \(n > 1\).

    Sequence Explicit Rule¶

    An explicit rule gives the \(n\)th term of a sequence directly as a function of \(n\), without needing to know the previous terms. For arithmetic sequences, the explicit rule is \(a_n = a_1 + (n - 1)d\). For geometric sequences, the explicit rule is \(a_n = a_1 \cdot r^{n-1}\).

    Chapter Review and Key Takeaways¶

    1. Algebraic expressions consist of variables, constants, coefficients, and operations. Understanding the parts of an expression is essential for manipulating and solving equations.

    2. The order of operations (PEMDAS) ensures that everyone simplifies expressions the same way and gets the same result.

    3. Variables represent unknown quantities, and coefficients multiply variables. Constant terms have fixed values.

    4. Expanding and factoring are inverse operations that help us rewrite expressions in equivalent forms.

    5. Integer exponents and laws of exponents provide rules for working with powers. Rational exponents connect exponent notation to radical notation.

    6. Equations state that two expressions are equal. One-step, two-step, and multi-step equations require progressively more operations to solve. Literal equations and formula rearrangement involve isolating a specific variable.

    7. Polynomials consist of terms with variables raised to nonnegative integer exponents. Adding, subtracting, and multiplying polynomials follows the same principles as working with numbers, with attention to like terms. Factoring breaks polynomials into simpler factors.

    8. Quadratic functions have graphs that are parabolas. They can be solved by factoring, square roots, completing the square, or the quadratic formula. Linear, quadratic, and exponential functions model different types of real-world relationships.

    9. Statistics involves displaying data, measuring correlation, and comparing models. Arithmetic and geometric sequences model situations with constant difference or constant ratio.

    Status: Specified
    Bloom Level: Apply
    Bloom Verb: solve
    Learning Objective: The learner will solve one-step, two-step, and multi-step linear equations.

    Prerequisites: Solve One-Step Equation, Solve Two-Step Equation, Solve Multi-Step Equation

    Evidence of Mastery: The learner correctly solves a given one-step, two-step, or multi-step linear equation, and explains each step using inverse operations.

    Misconceptions: The learner may apply operations in the wrong order, or forget to apply the operation to both sides of the equation.

    Instructional Rationale: Solving equations is a core algebra skill that builds on understanding of inverse operations and the balance model of equations. Learners need repeated practice with immediate feedback to develop fluency.

    Content: The sim presents 10 linear equations of increasing complexity: one-step equations with addition/subtraction, one-step equations with multiplication/division, two-step equations, and multi-step equations with distribution and combining like terms. The learner solves each equation by entering the step-by-step solution. The sim tracks the number of attempts and provides feedback after each question.

    Feedback: Correct responses receive positive feedback that confirms the answer. Incorrect responses receive corrective feedback that identifies the error and shows the correct step.

    Starting State: The learner sees the title "Equation Solver" and a prompt to begin with a one-step equation.

    Chapter Anchors: The chapter prose states that "To solve a one-step equation, we perform the inverse operation on both sides. To solve a two-step equation, we undo addition or subtraction first, then multiplication or division. To solve a multi-step equation, we simplify each side first, then use inverse operations in reverse order of the order of operations." These anchors must match the spec exactly.

    Chapter Practice and Exercises¶

    Exercise Set 1: Identifying Expression Parts¶

    For each expression, identify the coefficient, variable, constant, and number of terms.

    1. \(5x + 3\)
    2. \(7y - 2\)
    3. \(x + 4\)
    4. \(-3a + 2b - 1\)

    Expand each expression using the distributive property.

    1. \(2(x + 4)\)
    2. \(3(y - 5)\)
    3. \(x(y + 2)\)

    Evaluate each expression for the given value of the variable.

    1. \(3x + 2\) when \(x = 4\)

    3. \(2x^2\) when \(x = 3\)
    4. \(4y / 2\) when \(y = 6\)

    Chapter Summary¶

    In this chapter, we have established the foundational concepts of algebra that will support all future work in the course. Students should now be able to:

    • Identify the parts of an algebraic expression (variables, coefficients, constants, terms)
    • Apply the order of operations to simplify expressions
    • Expand and factor algebraic expressions
    • Work with integer, rational, and negative exponents using the laws of exponents
    • Solve one-step, two-step, and multi-step linear equations
    • Rearrange literal equations and formulas to solve for a specific variable
    • Add, subtract, multiply, and factor polynomials
    • Solve quadratic equations by factoring, square roots, completing the square, and the quadratic formula
    • Graph and analyze quadratic functions, including identifying the vertex and axis of symmetry
    • Model linear, quadratic, and exponential relationships from data
    • Work with arithmetic and geometric sequences
    • Create and interpret basic statistical displays and measure correlation

    Students who master these concepts are prepared for success in subsequent chapters covering functions, systems of equations, more advanced polynomial work, and introductory trigonometry. Continued practice with the exercise sets and MicroSims will build fluency and confidence for applying these skills to complex, real-world problems.
