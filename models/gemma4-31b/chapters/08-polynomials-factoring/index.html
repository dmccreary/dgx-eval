    Polynomials and Factoring¶

    Summary¶

    Students will learn to perform operations on polynomials and master various factoring techniques, such as GCF and trinomial factoring, which are essential for solving higher-degree equations.

    The Building Blocks of Polynomials¶

    Before we can add or multiply complex algebraic expressions, we need to understand the "anatomy" of the expressions we are working with. In the previous chapters, we worked with simple terms like \( 3x \) or \( 5y^2 \). When we combine these into larger structures, we enter the world of polynomials.

    Monomials: The Single-Term Expression¶

    A monomial is the simplest form of an algebraic expression. It consists of a single term that is either a number, a variable, or a product of numbers and variables with non-negative integer exponents. For example, \( 7 \), \( x \), and \( -4.5x^2y \) are all monomials. Crucially, a monomial cannot have a variable in the denominator or a negative exponent, as that would move the expression out of the realm of polynomials.

    To understand how a monomial works, we look at its components: the coefficient (the number multiplying the variable) and the variable part. In the monomial \( -5x^3 \), the coefficient is \( -5 \) and the variable part is \( x^3 \).

    Binomials and Trinomials¶

    When we add or subtract two or more monomials, we create a polynomial. Depending on how many non-zero terms are combined, we give them specific names:

    1. Binomial: A polynomial with exactly two terms. Example: \( 2x + 5 \) or \( x^2 - 9 \).
    2. Trinomial: A polynomial with exactly three terms. Example: \( x^2 + 5x + 6 \) or \( 3y^2 - 2y + 1 \).

    It is important to remember that we only count "like terms." If you have \( 2x + 3x + 5 \), this is not a trinomial; it simplifies to \( 5x + 5 \), which is a binomial.

    The General Polynomial¶

    A polynomial is the general term for an expression consisting of one or more terms. Formally, it is a sum of monomials. While we have special names for 1, 2, and 3 terms, any expression with more than three terms is simply called a polynomial.

    Degree and Leading Coefficients¶

    To describe the "size" and "behavior" of a polynomial, we use two key measurements: the degree and the leading coefficient.

    Degree of a Polynomial: The degree is the highest exponent of the variable in the polynomial. If the polynomial is a monomial, the degree is simply the exponent of that variable. For polynomials with multiple terms, the degree is the degree of the term with the highest exponent.

    Example: In \( 7x^4 - 3x^2 + 2 \), the terms have exponents 4, 2, and 0. The highest is 4, so the degree is 4.

    Leading Coefficient: The leading coefficient is the coefficient of the term with the highest degree. To find it, you must first write the polynomial in standard form (terms ordered from highest degree to lowest degree).

    Example: In \( 2 + 5x^3 - 4x \), the standard form is \( 5x^3 - 4x + 2 \). The term with the highest degree is \( 5x^3 \), so the leading coefficient is 5.

    Adding and Subtracting Polynomials¶

    Adding and subtracting polynomials is essentially an exercise in organizing and combining like terms. We treat the polynomial as a collection of terms and group those that have the exact same variable part.

    Adding Polynomials¶

    To add two polynomials, you simply remove the parentheses and combine like terms. Because we are adding, the signs of the terms in the second polynomial remain unchanged.

    The process is: 1. Identify like terms (terms with the same variable and exponent). 2. Sum their coefficients. 3. Write the final result in standard form.

    Worked Example: Addition Problem: Add \( (3x^2 - 5x + 8) \) and \( (2x^2 + 7x - 10) \). Solution: 1. Group like terms: \( (3x^2 + 2x^2) + (-5x + 7x) + (8 - 10) \). 2. Combine: \( 5x^2 + 2x - 2 \). 3. The result is already in standard form: \( 5x^2 + 2x - 2 \).

    Subtracting Polynomials¶

    Subtracting polynomials requires one extra, critical step: distributing the negative sign. Subtracting a polynomial is the same as adding its opposite.

    The process is: 1. Distribute the minus sign to every term inside the second polynomial (flip all the signs). 2. Combine like terms as you would in addition. 3. Write the result in standard form.

    Worked Example: Subtraction Problem: Subtract \( (x^2 - 4x + 2) \) from \( (3x^2 + 2x - 5) \). Solution: 1. Set up the expression: \( (3x^2 + 2x - 5) - (x^2 - 4x + 2) \). 2. Distribute the negative: \( 3x^2 + 2x - 5 - x^2 + 4x - 2 \). 3. Group like terms: \( (3x^2 - x^2) + (2x + 4x) + (-5 - 2) \). 4. Combine: \( 2x^2 + 6x - 7 \).

    Multiplying Polynomials¶

    While adding and subtracting only involve combining like terms, multiplication requires a different approach. Every term in the first polynomial must be multiplied by every term in the second polynomial.

    Multiplying Monomials¶

    Multiplying a monomial by another monomial is the most basic form. We use the Product Rule for Exponents (from Chapter 2) and multiply the coefficients.

    The process is: 1. Multiply the coefficients. 2. Add the exponents of the same variables.

    Worked Example: Monomial Multiplication Problem: Multiply \( (4x^2y) \) by \( (-3xy^3) \). Solution: 1. Coefficients: \( 4 \cdot (-3) = -12 \). 2. x-variables: \( x^2 \cdot x^1 = x^{2+1} = x^3 \). 3. y-variables: \( y^1 \cdot y^3 = y^{1+3} = y^4 \). 4. Result: \( -12x^3y^4 \).

    Multiplying a Monomial by a Polynomial¶

    To multiply a single term by a polynomial, we use the Distributive Property.

    Worked Example: Distribution Problem: Multiply \( 3x^2 \) by \( (2x^2 - 5x + 4) \). Solution: 1. Distribute to 1st term: \( 3x^2 \cdot 2x^2 = 6x^4 \). 2. Distribute to 2nd term: \( 3x^2 \cdot (-5x) = -15x^3 \). 3. Distribute to 3rd term: \( 3x^2 \cdot 4 = 12x^2 \). 4. Final result: \( 6x^4 - 15x^3 + 12x^2 \).

    Multiplying Two Binomials: The FOIL Method¶

    When multiplying two binomials, the distributive property can feel tedious. Instead, we use the FOIL mnemonic to ensure we don't miss any pairs.

    FOIL stands for: - First: Multiply the first terms in each binomial. - Outer: Multiply the outermost terms. - Inner: Multiply the innermost terms. - Last: Multiply the last terms in each binomial.

    Worked Example: FOIL Problem: Multiply \( (x + 3)(x - 5) \). Solution: - First: \( x \cdot x = x^2 \) - Outer: \( x \cdot (-5) = -5x \) - Inner: \( 3 \cdot x = 3x \) - Last: \( 3 \cdot (-5) = -15 \) - Combine all: \( x^2 - 5x + 3x - 15 \). - Simplify like terms: \( x^2 - 2x - 15 \).

    General Polynomial Multiplication¶

    To multiply a polynomial by another polynomial (beyond binomials), the FOIL method is no longer sufficient. Instead, we use the general distributive property: multiply every term in the first polynomial by every term in the second polynomial, then combine like terms.

    Worked Example: Polynomial Multiplication Problem: Multiply \( (x^2 - 2x + 3)(x - 4) \). Solution: 1. Multiply \( x \) by every term in the first: \( x(x^2) + x(-2x) + x(3) = x^3 - 2x^2 + 3x \). 2. Multiply \( -4 \) by every term in the first: \( -4(x^2) + -4(-2x) + -4(3) = -4x^2 + 8x - 12 \). 3. Combine the results: \( x^3 - 2x^2 - 4x^2 + 3x + 8x - 12 \). 4. Simplify like terms: \( x^3 - 6x^2 + 11x - 12 \).

    The Art of Factoring¶

    If multiplying polynomials is like expanding a building, factoring is like taking it apart to see the original blueprints. Factoring is the process of breaking down a polynomial into a product of simpler polynomials. This is a critical skill because it allows us to solve complex equations by finding the "roots" or "zeros" of the expression.

    The Greatest Common Factor (GCF)¶

    The first step in any factoring problem is always to look for the Greatest Common Factor (GCF). The GCF is the largest expression that divides evenly into every term of the polynomial.

    To find the GCF: 1. Find the largest number that divides all coefficients. 2. Identify the variables that appear in every term and take the one with the smallest exponent. 3. Combine these to create the GCF.

    Worked Example: Finding the GCF Problem: Find the GCF of \( 12x^4y^2 \), \( -18x^3y^3 \), and \( 6x^2y^5 \). Solution: 1. Coefficients: The GCF of 12, 18, and 6 is 6. 2. Variable x: The lowest power is \( x^2 \). 3. Variable y: The lowest power is \( y^2 \). 4. The GCF is \( 6x^2y^2 \).

    Factoring Out the GCF¶

    Once the GCF is identified, we "factor it out" by dividing every term in the polynomial by the GCF and placing the GCF outside a set of parentheses.

    Worked Example: Factoring GCF Problem: Factor \( 6x^3 + 12x^2 - 18x \). Solution: 1. Find the GCF: The GCF of 6, 12, and -18 is 6. The lowest power of x is \( x^1 \). So, GCF = \( 6x \). 2. Divide each term: - \( 6x^3 \div 6x = x^2 \) - \( 12x^2 \div 6x = 2x \) - \( -18x \div 6x = -3 \) 3. Write as a product: \( 6x(x^2 + 2x - 3) \).

    Factoring Trinomials (Quadratic Expressions)¶

    A trinomial of the form \( ax^2 + bx + c \) is a quadratic expression. Factoring these is essentially the reverse of the FOIL method.

    Case 1: Simple Trinomials (\( a = 1 \))¶

    When the leading coefficient is 1, we look for two numbers that multiply to \( c \) and add to \( b \).

    Worked Example: Factoring \( a=1 \) Problem: Factor \( x^2 + 7x + 10 \). Solution: 1. Find factors of 10 that add to 7. - 1 and 10 (add to 11) - No - 2 and 5 (add to 7) - Yes! 2. Write as binomials: \( (x + 2)(x + 5) \).

    Case 2: Complex Trinomials (\( a \neq 1 \))¶

    When \( a \) is not 1, we often use the AC Method (or grouping method).

    The AC Method process: 1. Multiply \( a \) and \( c \) (the "AC" product). 2. Find two numbers that multiply to \( AC \) and add to \( b \). 3. Split the middle term \( bx \) using these two numbers. 4. Factor by grouping.

    Worked Example: Factoring \( a \neq 1 \) Problem: Factor \( 2x^2 + 7x + 3 \). Solution: 1. AC product: \( 2 \cdot 3 = 6 \). 2. Factors of 6 that add to 7: 6 and 1. 3. Split the middle term: \( 2x^2 + 6x + 1x + 3 \). 4. Factor by grouping: - Group 1: \( 2x^2 + 6x = 2x(x + 3) \) - Group 2: \( 1x + 3 = 1(x + 3) \) 5. Result: \( (2x + 1)(x + 3) \).

    Factoring by Grouping¶

    When a polynomial has four terms, it often cannot be factored using the GCF or the AC method alone. Instead, we use factoring by grouping. This involves splitting the polynomial into two pairs, factoring each pair separately, and then identifying a common binomial factor.

    The process is: 1. Group the first two terms and the last two terms. 2. Factor out the GCF from the first pair. 3. Factor out the GCF from the second pair. 4. If the remaining binomials are identical, factor that binomial out.

    Worked Example: Factoring by Grouping Problem: Factor \( x^3 + 3x^2 + 4x + 12 \). Solution: 1. Group: \( (x^3 + 3x^2) + (4x + 12) \). 2. Factor first pair: \( x^2(x + 3) \). 3. Factor second pair: \( 4(x + 3) \). 4. Final Result: \( (x^2 + 4)(x + 3) \).

    Special Factoring Patterns¶

    Some polynomials follow specific mathematical patterns that allow us to factor them instantly without using the GCF or grouping methods.

    1. Difference of Two Squares¶

    A Difference of Two Squares is a binomial where both terms are perfect squares and they are subtracted. It always factors into the product of a sum and a difference of the square roots. Formula: \( a^2 - b^2 = (a - b)(a + b) \)

    Worked Example: Problem: Factor \( x^2 - 49 \). Solution: 1. Identify square roots: \( \sqrt{x^2} = x \), \( \sqrt{49} = 7 \). 2. Apply formula: \( (x - 7)(x + 7) \).

    2. Perfect Square Trinomials¶

    A Perfect Square Trinomial is the result of squaring a binomial. It has the form \( a^2 + 2ab + b^2 \) or \( a^2 - 2ab + b^2 \). Formula: \( a^2 \pm 2ab + b^2 = (a \pm b)^2 \)

    Worked Example: Problem: Factor \( x^2 + 10x + 25 \). Solution: 1. Check if first and last terms are squares: \( \sqrt{x^2} = x \), \( \sqrt{25} = 5 \). 2. Check if middle term is \( 2 \cdot x \cdot 5 = 10x \). Yes. 3. Result: \( (x + 5)^2 \).

    Completely Factoring¶

    In many cases, a polynomial requires multiple steps to be completely factored. The golden rule is: Always factor out the GCF first, then check for special patterns or trinomial factoring.

    Worked Example: Complete Factoring Problem: Factor completely \( 2x^3 - 8x \). Solution: 1. Factor GCF: GCF is \( 2x \). Result: \( 2x(x^2 - 4) \). 2. Check remaining binomial: \( x^2 - 4 \) is a Difference of Two Squares. 3. Factor the binomial: \( (x - 2)(x + 2) \). 4. Final Result: \( 2x(x - 2)(x + 2) \).

    Factoring to Solve Equations¶

    The ultimate purpose of factoring in Algebra I is to solve equations. This is made possible by the Zero Product Property.

    Zero Product Property: If the product of two or more factors is zero, then at least one of the factors must be zero. If \( A \cdot B = 0 \), then \( A = 0 \) or \( B = 0 \).

    The process for solving equations by factoring: 1. Set the equation to zero (all terms on one side). 2. Factor the polynomial completely. 3. Set each factor equal to zero. 4. Solve the resulting simple equations.

    Worked Example: Solving by Factoring Problem: Solve \( x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0 \). Solution: 1. Factor the trinomial: \( (x - 2)(x - 3) = 0 \). 2. Apply Zero Product Property: - \( x - 2 = 0 \rightarrow x = 2 \) - \( x - 3 = 0 \rightarrow x = 3 \) 3. Solutions: \( x = \{2, 3\} \).

    Summary and Key Takeaways¶

    Factoring is the essential process of decomposing complex polynomials into simpler products. By mastering the GCF, trinomial patterns, and special identities, we can simplify expressions and solve quadratic equations efficiently.

    Key Checklist for Factoring: - [ ] Did I factor out the GCF first? - [ ] If there are 2 terms, is it a Difference of Two Squares? - [ ] If there are 3 terms, is it a Perfect Square Trinomial or a standard trinomial? - [ ] If there are 4 terms, can I use grouping? - [ ] Did I factor completely (check if the results can be factored further)? - [ ] When solving, did I set the equation to 0 before factoring?
