    The Role of Variables in Functions¶

    Before we can analyze linear relationships, we must be clear about the roles that different variables play in a function. In any mathematical model, we are typically looking at how one quantity changes in response to another.

    Independent and Dependent Variables¶

    The Independent Variable is the input of a function. It is the variable that is changed or controlled in a mathematical model to see the effect on another variable. In a coordinate plane, the independent variable is almost always represented by the x-axis.

    Worked Example: Identifying the Independent Variable Imagine you are tracking the cost of a taxi ride. The cost depends on the number of miles you travel. - The number of miles is the independent variable. You can choose to travel 2 miles or 10 miles; the distance is the "cause" in the relationship.

    The Dependent Variable is the output of the function. Its value "depends" on the independent variable. In a coordinate plane, the dependent variable is represented by the y-axis.

    Worked Example: Identifying the Dependent Variable In the taxi ride example: - The total cost is the dependent variable. The price you pay is the "effect"—it changes based on how many miles you traveled.

    In the function notation \( f(x) = y \), \( x \) is the independent variable and \( y \) is the dependent variable.

    The Linear Function¶

    The most fundamental relationship in algebra is the linear relationship. A Linear Function is a function whose graph is a straight line. Algebraically, a linear function can be written in the form: [ f(x) = mx + b ] where \( m \) represents the slope and \( b \) represents the y-intercept. The defining characteristic of a linear function is that it has a constant rate of change. No matter which two points you pick on the line, the ratio of the change in \( y \) to the change in \( x \) is always the same.

    Worked Example: Linear vs. Non-Linear Which of the following is a linear function? 1. \( f(x) = 3x + 5 \) 2. \( g(x) = x^2 - 4 \) 3. \( h(x) = \frac{1}{x} \)

    • Analysis: In \( f(x) \), the variable \( x \) is raised to the first power. Its graph is a straight line. This is linear.
    • In \( g(x) \), the \( x^2 \) term creates a parabola (a curve). This is non-linear.
    • In \( h(x) \), the variable is in the denominator, creating a hyperbola. This is non-linear.

    Function Transformations¶

    In algebra, we often start with a simple "parent function" and modify it to create a new function. This process is called a Function Transformation. For linear functions, the parent function is \( f(x) = x \).

    Worked Example: Transforming the Parent Function If we start with \( f(x) = x \) and change it to \( g(x) = 2x + 3 \): 1. The multiplier 2 is a stretch, making the line steeper. 2. The addition of 3 is a shift, moving the entire line up by 3 units.

    Types of Transformations¶

    A Shift (or translation) moves the graph of a function vertically or horizontally without changing its shape. - Vertical shift: \( f(x) + k \) (moves up if \( k > 0 \)) - Horizontal shift: \( f(x - h) \) (moves right if \( h > 0 \))

    A Reflection flips the graph over an axis. - Reflection over the x-axis: \( -f(x) \) (the line's slope changes sign) - Reflection over the y-axis: \( f(-x) \)

    A Stretch pulls the graph away from an axis, making a linear function steeper. This happens when the slope is multiplied by a factor greater than 1.

    A Compression pushes the graph toward an axis, making a linear function flatter. This happens when the slope is multiplied by a factor between 0 and 1.

    Special Linear Variations¶

    While most linear functions are diagonal, some take special forms: - Constant Function: A function where the output is the same for every input (e.g., \( f(x) = 5 \)). Its graph is a horizontal line. - Step Function: A piecewise-linear function that looks like a series of stairs, where the value remains constant over intervals then "jumps" to a new value. - Piecewise Function: A function defined by multiple different rules for different parts of its domain. For example, a function might be linear for \( x < 0 \) and constant for \( x \ge 0 \).

    Slope and Rate of Change¶

    The most important property of a linear function is its Rate of Change. In a real-world context, this is the ratio of the change in the dependent variable to the change in the independent variable.

    Worked Example: Calculating Rate of Change A plant grows from 2 cm to 10 cm over 4 days. - Change in height (\( \Delta y \)): \( 10 - 2 = 8 \text{ cm} \) - Change in time (\( \Delta x \)): \( 4 - 0 = 4 \text{ days} \) - Rate of Change: \( \frac{8 \text{ cm}}{4 \text{ days}} = 2 \text{ cm/day} \)

    In mathematics, we call this rate of change the Slope. The slope describes the steepness and direction of a line.

    Worked Example: Understanding Slope Direction - Positive Slope: As \( x \) increases, \( y \) also increases (the line goes "up" from left to right). - Negative Slope: As \( x \) increases, \( y \) decreases (the line goes "down" from left to right).

    The Algebra of Slope¶

    To find the slope of a line passing through two points \( (x_1, y_1) \) and \( (x_2, y_2) \), we use the Slope Formula: [ m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} ]

    Worked Example: Using the Slope Formula Find the slope of the line passing through \( (2, 5) \) and \( (4, 11) \). 1. Identify coordinates: \( x_1=2, y_1=5, x_2=4, y_2=11 \). 2. Substitute into formula: \( m = \frac{11 - 5}{4 - 2} \). 3. Simplify: \( m = \frac{6}{2} = 3 \). The slope is 3.

    Special Cases of Slope¶

    Not all lines have a standard diagonal slope: - Zero Slope: A horizontal line has a slope of 0. This happens when \( y_2 - y_1 = 0 \). The line is flat. - Undefined Slope: A vertical line has an undefined slope. This happens when \( x_2 - x_1 = 0 \), which would require division by zero in the formula.

    Equations of Lines¶

    Once we have the slope and a point, we can write an equation that describes the entire line.

    Slope-Intercept Form¶

    The most common form is the Slope-Intercept Form: [ y = mx + b ] - \( m \) is the Slope. - \( b \) is the Y-Intercept, which is the point where the line crosses the y-axis \( (0, b) \).

    Worked Example: Writing an Equation Write the equation of a line with a slope of \( -2 \) and a y-intercept of \( 4 \). - Substitute \( m = -2 \) and \( b = 4 \). - Equation: \( y = -2x + 4 \).

    The X-Intercept is the point where the line crosses the x-axis. To find it, set \( y = 0 \) and solve for \( x \). - For \( y = -2x + 4 \), set \( 0 = -2x + 4 \rightarrow 2x = 4 \rightarrow x = 2 \). The x-intercept is \( (2, 0) \).

    Other Linear Forms¶

    Depending on the information given, other forms are more useful:

    Point-Slope Form: Used when you have a point \( (x_1, y_1) \) and a slope \( m \): [ y - y_1 = m(x - x_1) ]

    Standard Form: Often used in systems of equations, where \( A, B, \) and \( C \) are integers: [ Ax + By = C ]

    Geometric Relationships¶

    Graphing a Line¶

    To Graph with Slope and Intercept, follow these steps: 1. Plot the y-intercept \( (0, b) \). 2. Use the slope \( m = \frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}}$ to find a second point. (e.g., if \( m = 2/3 \), go up 2 units and right 3 units). 3. Draw a straight line through the two points.

    Parallel and Perpendicular Lines¶

    Two lines in a plane can have specific geometric relationships based on their slopes.

    Parallel Lines are lines that never intersect. This occurs if and only if they have the same slope. - Line 1: \( y = 3x + 5 \) - Line 2: \( y = 3x - 2 \) Since both slopes are 3, these lines are parallel.

    Perpendicular Lines intersect at a right angle (\( 90^\circ \)). This occurs if their slopes are negative reciprocals of each other.

    The Negative Reciprocal of a number \( m \) is \( -1/m \). - If Line 1 has slope \( m = 2 \), a perpendicular line must have slope \( m = -1/2 \). - If Line 1 has slope \( m = -3/4 \), a perpendicular line must have slope \( m = 4/3 \).

    Chapter Summary¶

    In this chapter, we moved from the general concept of functions to the specific behavior of linear functions. Key takeaways include: - Variables: The independent variable (x) drives the change in the dependent variable (y). - Linear Functions: Defined by a constant rate of change, resulting in a straight-line graph. - Slope: The "steepness" of the line, calculated as rise over run (\( \Delta y / \Delta x \)). - Equations: Lines can be represented in Slope-Intercept (\( y = mx + b \)), Point-Slope, or Standard form. - Geometry: Parallel lines have equal slopes; perpendicular lines have negative reciprocal slopes.
