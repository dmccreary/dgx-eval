    Building Blocks: Constants and Coefficients¶

    To work effectively with algebra, we must first understand the different types of numbers that make up mathematical expressions. These are the fundamental units from which all complex equations and functions are constructed.

    Constants¶

    A constant is a value that does not change. In an algebraic expression, constants provide a fixed reference point. Unlike variables, which can take on many different values, a constant remains exactly what it is throughout any calculation.

    Constants can appear in many different forms. They might be simple integers like \( 5 \) or \( -12 \), or they might be fractions like \( \frac{3}{4} \). They can also be irrational numbers that have infinite, non-repeating decimals, such as \( \pi \) (approximately \( 3.14159 \)) or \( \sqrt{2} \). No matter how much the rest of the expression changes, the constant is "constant."

    Worked Example: Identifying Constants

    Let's examine three different expressions to find the constants:

    1. Expression: \( 7x + 5 \)
    2. Here, \( x \) is the variable.

    3. The number \( 5 \) is the constant because its value is always five.

    4. Expression: \( y - \frac{2}{3} \)

    5. The variable is \( y \).

    6. The constant is \( -\frac{2}{3} \).

    7. Expression: \( 4a^2 + \pi \)

    8. The variable is \( a \).
    9. The constant is \( \pi \).

    Coefficients¶

    A coefficient is a numerical or constant quantity placed before and multiplying a variable in an algebraic expression. It tells us "how many" of the variable we have.

    The coefficient essentially acts as a multiplier. In the term \( 6x \), the number \( 6 \) is the coefficient of \( x \). This tells us that we are taking the value of \( x \) and multiplying it by six.

    It is important to remember two special cases: 1. The Invisible One: When a variable appears alone, such as \( y \), its coefficient is implicitly \( 1 \). Similarly, \( -z \) has a coefficient of \( -1 \). 2. Fractional Coefficients: A coefficient can be a fraction, such as \( \frac{1}{2}x \), meaning we are taking half of the value of \( x \).

    Worked Example: Finding Coefficients

    Identify the coefficients in the following expressions:

    1. \( -12m \)
    2. The coefficient is \( -12 \).
    3. \( \frac{5}{8}k \)
    4. The coefficient is \( \frac{5}{8} \).
    5. \( w + 10 \)
    6. The coefficient of \( w \) is \( 1 \).
    7. \( -p \)
    8. The coefficient of \( p \) is \( -1 \).

    Exponents and Roots¶

    Once we understand the building blocks, we can explore how numbers can be scaled and transformed using exponents and roots. These operations allow us to express very large or very small quantities efficiently.

    Integer Exponents¶

    An exponent (also called a power) indicates how many times a base is multiplied by itself. When we use integer exponents, we are dealing with whole numbers (positive, negative, or zero) as our powers.

    The expression \( b^n \) tells us to take the base \( b \) and multiply it by itself \( n \) times.

    The Rules of Exponents:

    1. Positive Exponents: If \( n > 0 \), then \( b^n \) is \( b \) multiplied by itself \( n \) times.
    2. Example: \( 3^4 = 3 \times 3 \times 3 \times 3 = 81 \)
    3. Zero Exponent: If \( n = 0 \) and \( b \neq 0 \), then \( b^0 = 1 \). This rule is vital for simplifying complex expressions.
    4. Example: \( 525^0 = 1 \)
    5. Negative Exponents: If \( n < 0 \), then \( b^n = \frac{1}{b^{-n}} \). A negative exponent indicates the reciprocal of the base raised to the positive version of that exponent. It is a way of saying "divide by the base this many times."
    6. Example: \( 2^{-3} = \frac{1}{2^3} = \frac{1}{8} \)

    Worked Example: Calculating with Integer Exponents

    Solve the following:

    1. \( 10^3 \)
    2. \( 10 \times 10 \times 10 = 1,000 \)
    3. \( ( \frac{2}{3} )^0 \)
    4. Any non-zero number to the power of zero is \( 1 \).
    5. \( 6^{-2} \)
    6. \( \frac{1}{6^2} = \frac{1}{36} \)

    Exponential Decay¶

    Exponential decay is a process where a quantity decreases at a rate proportional to its current value. This often results in the quantity getting smaller and smaller, approaching zero but never quite reaching it. In algebra, we model this using a base between 0 and 1.

    The "decay factor" is the base of the exponent. If a quantity decreases by \( 20\% \), the decay factor is \( 1 - 0.20 = 0.80 \).

    Worked Example: Modeling Decay

    Imagine you have \( 500 \) mg of a substance in a lab, and it decays by \( 15\% \) every hour. This means \( 85\% \) (or \( 0.85 \)) remains each hour.

    The amount remaining after \( t \) hours can be modeled by: [ A(t) = 500(0.85)^t ]

    • After 1 hour: \( 500(0.85)^1 = 425 \) mg
    • After 2 hours: \( 500(0.85)^2 = 361.25 \) mg

    nth Roots¶

    The nth root of a number is a value that, when multiplied by itself \( n \) times, equals the original number. This is the inverse operation of raising a number to the \( n^{th} \) power.

    Worked Example: Finding Roots

    1. Square Root (\( n=2 \)): \( \sqrt{144} = 12 \) (since \( 12 \times 12 = 144 \))
    2. Cube Root (\( n=3 \)): \( \sqrt[3]{125} = 5 \) (since \( 5 \times 5 \times 5 = 125 \))
    3. Fifth Root (\( n=5 \)): \( \sqrt[5]{32} = 2 \) (since \( 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 = 32 \))

    Patterns and Statistics¶

    Beyond individual numbers, algebra helps us describe patterns in numbers and summarize large sets of data.

    Common Ratio¶

    In a geometric sequence, a common ratio is the constant factor used to get from one term to the next. You can find it by dividing any term by the term before it.

    Example: In the sequence \( 5, 15, 45, 135, \dots \), the common ratio is \( 3 \) because \( 15/5 = 3 \) and \( 45/15 = 3 \).

    Arithmetic Mean¶

    The arithmetic mean (often simply called the "average") is a single value that represents the "center" of a set of numbers. To find it, add all the numbers in a set together and divide the sum by the total count of numbers in that set.

    Example: The mean of \( 12, 15, \) and \( 21 \) is \( \frac{12+15+21}{3} = \frac{48}{3} = 16 \).

    Functions and Linear Relationships¶

    Functions and linear equations allow us to model relationships between changing quantities.

    Slope-Intercept Form¶

    A linear equation can be written in the slope-intercept form: [ y = mx + b ]

    Where: - \( m \) is the slope, representing the "rise over run" (the rate of change). - \( b \) is the y-intercept, the point where the line crosses the vertical y-axis.

    Worked Example: Graphing from an Equation

    For the equation \( y = -\frac{2}{3}x + 5 \): 1. The y-intercept is \( 5 \), so start at the point \( (0, 5) \). 2. The slope is \( -\frac{2}{3} \), meaning "down 2, right 3". Move from \( (0, 5) \) to \( (3, 3) \).

    Range of a Function¶

    When we talk about a function, we focus on two sets of values: the inputs (domain) and the outputs (range). The range is the set of all possible output values.

    Example: For the function \( f(x) = |x| \), the range is all non-negative numbers (\( y \geq 0 \)) because an absolute value can never be negative.

    Step Functions¶

    A step function is a piecewise function that consists of constant segments, creating a staircase-like appearance on a graph. These functions are useful for modeling things that change in discrete jumps, like postage rates or parking garage fees.

    Example: If a parking garage charges $5 for the first hour and $10 for the second hour, the cost "jumps" at the one-hour mark.

    Equations and Systems¶

    Finally, we use algebra to solve for unknown values in complex situations.

    Multi-Step Equations¶

    A multi-step equation is an equation that requires several algebraic operations (like combining like terms, using the distributive property, or moving variables) to isolate the variable.

    To solve these, you must follow the "Golden Rule of Algebra": Whatever you do to one side of the equation, you must do to the other. This maintains the equality.

    The Process of Solving:

    1. Simplify: Use the distributive property to remove parentheses and combine like terms on each side.
    2. Isolate the Variable Term: Use addition or subtraction to move all terms containing the variable to one side and all constant terms to the other.
    3. Solve for the Variable: Use multiplication or division to get the variable by itself.
    4. Verify: Plug your answer back into the original equation to ensure it works.

    Worked Example: A Complex Multi-Step Equation

    Solve for \( x \): [ 4(x - 3) + 2x = 2(x + 5) ]

    1. Simplify (Distribute): [ 4x - 12 + 2x = 2x + 10 ]
    2. Simplify (Combine Like Terms on the left): [ 6x - 12 = 2x + 10 ]
    3. Isolate (Subtract \( 2x \) from both sides): [ 4x - 12 = 10 ]
    4. Isolate (Add \( 12 \) to both sides): [ 4x = 22 ]
    5. Solve (Divide by 4): [ x = \frac{22}{4} = 5.5 ]

    Graphing Inequalities¶

    An inequality is a mathematical statement that compares two expressions, showing if one is less than, greater than, or not equal to the other. Graphing inequalities involves shading a region on a coordinate plane that represents all the possible solutions.

    Definition: Graphing an inequality is the process of representing the set of all points that make an inequality true on a graph.

    If the inequality is \( y > x \), we use a dashed line for the boundary. If it is \( y \geq x \), we use a solid line.

    Graphing Systems¶

    A system of equations is a set of two or more equations that you deal with at the same time. The solution to the system is the point (or region) where the graphs of the equations overlap.

    Worked Example: Finding the Intersection

    Consider the system: 1. \( y = 2x - 1 \) 2. \( y = -x + 5 \)

    1. Graph the first line: Start at \( (0, -1) \) and use a slope of \( 2 \) (up 2, right 1).
    2. Graph the second line: Start at \( (0, 5) \) and use a slope of \( -1 \) (down 1, right 1).
    3. Find the intersection: The two lines cross at the point \( (2, 3) \).
    4. Solution: The solution to the system is the coordinate \( (2, 3) \).
